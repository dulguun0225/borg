# Gates

Where a decision about a change happens, what may be decided there, and what decides whether a human decides at all.

## Where a gate is, and what decides it

There is a gate at every [stage](01-one-pipeline.md) boundary: after [decomposition](02-intent-into-items.md#decomposition) into [items](01-one-pipeline.md), after the spec, implementation plan, tasks, and implementation are authored, and before each merge and each deploy. Two kinds: an **artifact gate** decides over a document a stage has written, and an **event gate** decides whether an event — a merge, a deploy — happens at all, which is what makes [hold](#what-a-gate-may-change) a stop rather than an undo. The mechanism is permanent — it does not weaken as the factory improves.

The factory scores each change and auto-passes what it judges low risk. The same [score](04-risk-score.md) picks the [_rollout strategy_](#the-rollout-strategy). Humans override by adding a safeguard — a gate held always-on, or a strategy that keeps a [control](08-operations.md#the-health-monitor) — and can undo a change after it shipped.

A gate **firing** — one gate deciding one thing at one moment — writes one **decision** into the append-only log seam 2 of [_Deferred, but not designed out_](../deferred.md) describes, which is that log's single [writer](../what-the-factory-does.md#tight-integration) with the gate component as its caller. A decision is two rows and not one: an **open event** appended when the gate fires, and a **close event** appended when the **verdict** is given — approve, reject, or hold — naming the row it closes. A row is chained when it is appended, so a verdict written onto the open event afterwards would be a rewrite of a chained record — the rewrite [_Drift detection_](08-operations.md#drift-detection)'s check on the chain exists to catch, made the ordinary path for every gate in the factory. Two appends is what a [_page_](08-operations.md#pages) already does for the same reason: a page is a sequence of rows about one wait, each saying which of them it is. The [_deploy record_](06-releases.md#the-deploy-record) advances in place instead, and that is not a counterexample — it is an ordinary record rather than a row of the log, and nothing chains it. What forces the write at the firing is the [factor vector](04-risk-score.md#factors-at-least): a human is meant to argue with the score's factors before deciding, so the vector has to exist while they are deciding, and it cannot be computed when they open the row because the score version moves as outcomes arrive. Opened, it names the gate row, the item, the [build](05-environments.md#records-and-one-long-lived-branch) where one exists, the [artifact version](01-one-pipeline.md) under decision at an artifact gate, the vector and the number, the [policy version](../what-the-factory-does.md#traceability) and the [score version](04-risk-score.md#factors-at-least), the values actually applied, and the [duty](../what-humans-do.md) or named human the row waits on. The artifact version is there because the record is open across two events and artifacts are editable at a gate: [Edit in place](#actions-at-each-gate) authors a new version through the [artifact store](01-one-pipeline.md) while the row is still waiting, so a verdict that named only the item would point at whatever the artifact says when someone reads it rather than at what was decided over. Naming it costs one field, the version chain already existing; what it costs a reader is that an approval names a version rather than an artifact, so asking what was approved reads the chain rather than the current artifact. At Decomposition it names the [intent](02-intent-into-items.md#intake) and the set decomposition proposed rather than one item, that row being the one gate above a [timeline](11-screens.md#work-ops-factory-people) rather than on it. At [_A role prompt or a skill_](#a-role-prompt-or-a-skill) it names the version under decision and no item at all, that row belonging to no timeline, no [service](02-intent-into-items.md#decomposition), and no build. The close event names the open event, the verdict, its actor, when that actor opened the row where the actor is a human, and where the verdict sends an item back [what it returns to](#going-back-up), and names neither the policy version nor the score version: what a decision was made under is a fact of the firing, so it is written once where the firing is and two rows cannot come to disagree about it. Both are written against the item and the build and never against the [release](06-releases.md#the-release-record), including at the production deploy gate, which fires after the release exists — one rule for all eight rows, so no reader has to know which side of the merge a gate is on. What that costs is that a decision is read by joining two rows rather than by reading one, and that a pending gate is an open event with no close event yet — which is how [_Work_](11-screens.md#work-ops-factory-people) already describes it. An item stopped at [_the attempt limit_](#the-attempt-limit) leaves an open event that never receives its close event, so absence of a verdict is not by itself evidence that anyone is still deciding. An open event admits exactly one close event — the log's writer refuses a second close naming an open event already closed — so where [a duty is held by more than one human](08-operations.md#pages) the first verdict decides and a later one is refused, and the refusal is a state the screen shows: the row already decided, with its verdict, never an error about nothing. And [Edit in place](#actions-at-each-gate) while the row waits re-fires the gate rather than leaving the verdict to bind to a version the human rejected by editing it: authoring the new version appends a new open event naming it, with a vector computed from what is now under decision, and the superseded open event never receives its close — the reading the attempt limit's abandoned rows already have. The row an edit fires waits on the human whose edit fired it, an edit not being an approval, so a version a human wrote at a gate is decided by one however the number moves. What the pair costs is that approving after editing is two rows where it reads as one action, and that open events accumulate without closes wherever an edit, a supersession, or an escalation moved the decision elsewhere.

A human at a gate is an instrument, and the design reads it rather than assumes it. Three things follow. The vector is shown while the human decides and the number only beside the verdict once it is written: [_Implementation_](#implementation) derives an encoding's expected behaviour from the criterion's sentence and never from the code, because a reader of the code agrees with it, and a reader shown the score's conclusion first agrees with that — and a human at a gate already knows the number was above the [risk threshold](09-gate-policy.md#what-is-in-it), that being why they are there, so what withholding it costs is only how far above. The close event carries, beside its own time, when the actor opened the row in [Work](11-screens.md#work-ops-factory-people), written with Work as the caller so that it is the screen's report and not the human's, and [Factory](11-screens.md#work-ops-factory-people) reports how long a row was open in front of a human before its verdict, per duty and per named human, beside the wait it already reports. And the pair Factory reports for the factory — what it auto-approved and how often that was later undone (10) or rolled back — it reports per human the same way, over the rows each approved. Those are readings and not levers: nothing removes a human from a gate on a number, and a safeguard (9) adds one whatever the readings say. What they change is what a safeguard is understood to buy — a human added, whose detection is measured by the pair rather than assumed — so an owner can see a gate a human signs without reading: rows approved in the time the previous three hundred took, undone at the auto-pass rate. What it costs is a field a row left open in another tab overstates; a number per named human that reads as a measure of a person, kept because the alternative is a defence the design cites and never examines; and a residue the readings show and do not repair — duties 11 and 12 hand a human only what [the fleet](10-fleet.md) of agents failed at, on practice that shrinks as the factory improves, and nothing here keeps a human practised.

A failing rollout rolls back on its own, inside its [_analysis window_](08-operations.md#the-analysis-window) — no human involved, no waiting. The rollback is reported, not requested.

A human's undo (10) assumes the change can still be undone, and what a human may do narrows as later work builds on it: a rollback while the build it would return to is still running, a revert once it is not. Reversibility is a factor of the score and what a human may still do is limited by it, through the strategy it picks and the [instances of the build being replaced](08-operations.md#the-health-monitor) that strategy keeps running. It narrows that way and no other: with one item per release, a change is never harder to undo because it shipped alongside nine others. What overlapping analysis windows add is not difficulty but reach — a rollback undoes every release above its target, up to the [window limit](08-operations.md#overlapping-windows): the one set of items the factory ships together.

## The rollout strategy

A **rollout strategy** is how a release takes live traffic from the build it replaces. It attaches to a production deploy and to no other, which is narrower than [master](05-environments.md#records-and-one-long-lived-branch)-fed: what a strategy decides is whether a control runs, a control is the instances a comparison is made against, and that comparison exists only in production because **organic traffic** — the requests real users are already making — does. A customer's master-fed pre-production environment has no more of it than a [candidate](06-releases.md#what-a-build-is-called-and-when) one, so a strategy there would decide nothing that anything reads — the same division [_The health monitor_](08-operations.md#the-health-monitor) already draws.

| Strategy | How the release takes traffic |
|---|---|
| **with a control** | on a schedule, with the build it replaces serving the rest throughout — a share widened as the comparison stays clear, a share kept fixed while the two are compared, or all of it at once switched to a second complete copy running beside the one it replaces |
| **without a control** | all of it, in place, with none of the build it replaces left running |

Two rows and not four, because they differ on one axis and everything downstream reads that axis alone: whether the build being replaced is still serving while the release does. That is what a [_control_](08-operations.md#the-health-monitor) requires, and what a human undoing a change after it shipped (10) uses while it runs — the other row has neither. The schedule is an attribute of the first row rather than a strategy of its own. Canary, A/B, and blue-green are the three a builder arrives already knowing, and they are that row at three schedules. They differ in how much traffic the release is exposed to and not in what is provisioned: on every schedule the build being replaced keeps the instances it had until the [window](08-operations.md#overlapping-windows) that could return to it closes, because those instances are what a rollback shifts production onto and a share is not a capacity. The drawback of folding them in: a name a builder brings is now a value on a field instead of a row to implement.

Both rows replace instances, and neither drops a request doing it: the operation among the named ones of seam 4 of [_Deferred, but not designed out_](../deferred.md) that replaces an instance stops new requests reaching it and lets the ones it holds finish before it ends, and the [deployer](08-operations.md#the-health-monitor) advances a target only when that operation reports it done. That is the operation's contract and not a strategy, which is why it is not a third row — it holds on the row without a control, where all of production turns over in place, exactly as on the row with one. What it costs is a replacement taking as long as the longest request it waits on, and a platform unable to hold a request open across the replacement performing a cut the factory records as a drain. What neither row offers is a switch inside the build — code shipped dark and turned on later. A change turned on by a flag is a change that took no gate, reached no environment as the thing it is, and opened no [window](08-operations.md#the-analysis-window) when it started serving, which is the second path [_One pipeline_](01-one-pipeline.md) refuses; so the only correction after a window closes is a pipeline pass, behind [a queue whose order is not settable](05-environments.md#the-merge-queue) after the merge. That cost is stated plainly: an urgent fix waits its turn, and what shortens the turn is the size of the items ahead of it, not anything an owner can do at the moment it is needed.

A service's first release has no control whatever the score prefers: there is no build being replaced, so nothing can keep serving beside it and there is no control to run. The choice exists from the second release; what the first is measured against instead is in [_The health monitor_](08-operations.md#the-health-monitor).

The first row also makes a demand of the **platform** — whatever a service runs on, reached through seam 4 of [_Deferred, but not designed out_](../deferred.md) — and not every platform answers it. Serving a share means deciding what fraction of arriving traffic reaches each of two builds, which the named operations of seam 4 of [_Deferred, but not designed out_](../deferred.md) have to carry through to the target. A platform that moves instances rather than traffic — replacing them one at a time, or all at once, on its own schedule — cannot answer it: both builds run and nothing decides what each serves, so there is no comparison to make and nothing to shift back when it goes wrong. Where a service runs on one of those, the row is unavailable and every deploy goes without a control — the exemption a first release already takes, arriving here for a different reason and permanently rather than once. What that costs is that the score's choice of strategy is bounded by where a service happens to run: two services can be scored identically on one factory and roll out differently, and an owner reading a deploy without a control cannot tell a low number from a platform that offered nothing else. It is stated here rather than left to the [deployer](08-operations.md#the-health-monitor) to discover, because a strategy the platform cannot perform is a rollout reporting success having run no comparison at all.

## Actions at each gate

What may be done at each of the eight rows, and nothing about what decides it.

| Gate | Actions |
|---|---|
| Decomposition | Approve · Reject with feedback · Edit in place |
| Spec | Approve · Reject with feedback · Edit in place |
| Implementation plan | Approve · Reject with feedback · Edit in place |
| Tasks | Approve · Reject with feedback · Edit in place |
| Implementation | Approve · Reject with feedback |
| Deploy to candidate environment | Approve · Hold · Reject with feedback |
| Merge to master | Approve · Reject with feedback |
| Deploy to production | Approve · Hold · Safeguard the strategy |

Those eight rows are the default path, not the whole set. There is a gate before every deploy, so a customer that defines more environments gets a row for each. It gets no more merge rows: one long-lived branch is the promotion path, so the extra environments are deploy targets.

Two further rows are the factory's own and belong to no item: [_A role prompt or a skill_](#a-role-prompt-or-a-skill), where a version of what an agent is told is decided, and [_A safeguard's withdrawal_](#a-safeguards-withdrawal), where the one record that removes a human from a gate is decided. Each is outside the eight because everything the eight share is an item — a stage to be at, a build to point at, a release to reach — and that row has none of them.

They are fed from master, and the candidate-fed row stays the factory's own. A customer environment persists, and a persistent one fed from candidate builds is a slot two candidates take turns on — the shared environment [_An environment per candidate_](05-environments.md#an-environment-per-candidate) exists to remove, which delays everything behind whichever item is using it. A human who wants to see a pre-merge change sees it on that candidate's own environment, where a safeguard (9) puts them, and the delay is that item's alone. The drawback: no long-lived environment keeps a candidate running.

What actions a new row has follows from what feeds it, not from which row it was copied from. Fed from master, every new row has Approve and Hold for the reason Deploy to production has them: the merge has happened and the [release number](06-releases.md#the-release-number) is already assigned, so hold is the only stop and what is left is a human's undo of a shipped change (10). Reject is available up to the merge to master and nowhere after it.

That is the actions and only the actions: the [_strategy_](#the-rollout-strategy) and the [_analysis window_](08-operations.md#the-analysis-window) follow production rather than master, so a new row fed from master gets neither — the release number is already assigned, and what makes a control worth running is organic traffic the row does not have.

## What a gate may change

At a gate, artifacts are editable by hand. Code is not: a gate approves or rejects an implementation, it never hand-patches one. A human who wants different code authors it upstream and [sends the item back](#going-back-up) to have it built again.

Merge and deploy gates edit nothing — what they decide is an event, not a document. Reject sends the item back to a stage — the one its verdict names, and the nearest authoring stage above the row where it names none, which [_Going back up_](#going-back-up) sets out; hold leaves the event queued with the change still good, which is why only the deploy rows offer it. What a hold waits on differs by gate: at the production deploy gate the service's open windows already at its window limit, a rollback whose revert has not shipped, a declared dependency that is not live, or a record the [drift detector](08-operations.md#drift-detection) found disagreeing with what runs; at the candidate deploy gate a dependency that is not live yet — the producing release of a [contract migration](07-contracts.md#no-single-item-may-break-a-contract), since a candidate environment is composed from its dependencies' current releases and never from another service's candidate — or a platform with no room for another environment, the one condition here decomposition could not have declared. The two are different answers and have to stay distinguishable: the score learns from a reject and should learn nothing from a hold.

There are three kinds of hold, and only one is written as a decision. A hold a human sets is the verdict of that firing's decision record, with that human as the actor. A hold the factory sets over a record that already exists — an open window, an [error budget](08-operations.md#service-level-objectives) exhausted, a dependency that is not current, a rollback whose revert has not shipped, a mismatch the drift detector found — writes nothing and is recomputed at every firing, which is what checking at the moment of firing already requires; a record for it would be a decision where the design says nothing is decided, and re-testing would append one every time the gate re-fired. A wait the factory could not compute at a firing, because no gate fired or because the condition is not a record — a credential that cannot be reached, a platform with no room — is written into the log by the log, with the component that met it as the caller and the actor, so [Work](11-screens.md#work-ops-factory-people) has a row to show. None of the three counts against the attempt limit and none teaches the score. What that costs: the factory's own hold over a record that already exists is written nowhere, so how long it has been holding is answerable for the other two alone.

## The attempt limit

Every stage also has an attempt limit, authored with the rest of [gate policy](09-gate-policy.md) (8). An item that exceeds it stops being retried and appears in [_Work_](11-screens.md#work-ops-factory-people) as an escalation (12) — the factory saying it cannot do this one. What the limit is compared against is the item's own count for the stage it is at, which [_What an item names_](02-intent-into-items.md#what-an-item-names) keeps per stage and [dispatch](02-intent-into-items.md#dispatch) writes: counting the rejects in the log instead would miss every attempt that failed before a gate fired, which is exactly what a high limit spends [agent](01-one-pipeline.md) time on. A deploy is not an attempt: the [deployer](08-operations.md#the-health-monitor) performs it, no entry is dispatched to it, and dispatch writes no count for it. It is one parameter and not three: [_The interview_](02-intent-into-items.md#the-interview) counts its rounds against the same limit and decomposition counts its re-decompositions against it, both on the intent and both upstream of an item's first stage — the interview having no gate of its own, decomposition's gate deciding a set rather than an item — because a second row would be a different number on the same mechanism, a row every builder implements twice. Holds do not count against the limit; a hold is not a failed attempt, for the same reason the score does not learn from one. The limit is wrong in both directions: low turns solvable work into human work and stops an interview about to converge; high spends agent time before anyone sees the item. That spending is bounded by the [_spend ceiling_](10-fleet.md#a-spend-ceiling) where an owner authored one on the credential the agent runs on — a limit on units spent through it, reaching which is a hold — and by the quota on the [provider account](10-fleet.md#what-an-agent-runs-on) behind it otherwise. The two limits are one construction: this one counts attempts and the ceiling counts units, and each compares against the factory's own record rather than against anything at the provider.

## Going back up

Every stage above moves an item toward a release; this is the one way back. An item goes back three ways — a gate rejects it, the author working the stage sends it, or [the merge queue](05-environments.md#the-merge-queue) rejects a candidate that failed its own re-verification — and what happens to the work below is the same in each.

A reject names the stage the item returns to. By default that is the stage that fired — which is what a reject already meant: another attempt at the same artifact. At the two event gates that reject there is no stage of their own, so the default is the nearest authoring stage above, which is Implementation for both; the merge queue's rejection defaults there as well, and whichever of [its three readings](05-environments.md#the-merge-queue) it found — a candidate that no longer passes against what it will actually ship beside, or an encoding that could not hold its answer — names no stage above it. Decomposition names nothing at all: its reject re-decomposes the set rather than sending an item anywhere, so the field its close event would carry stays unwritten there. Where what is wrong is upstream, the verdict names the stage that owns it and the item goes there directly: an implementation rejected because the spec says two things returns to Spec, and Implementation plan and Tasks are not visited on the way. Nothing limits the jump to one step: a walk back one stage at a time arrives at the same place having paid an attempt and a rejected artifact at every stage in between. The latest anything may be sent back from is the merge to master — the ceiling reject already has, and for its reason: after the merge the release number is assigned and the release exists, so what is left is [undoing the change after it shipped](../what-humans-do.md) (10) and not a stage to re-author. Spec is the earliest of an item's own stages, and below it two things that are not among them may be named, because a defect above the item is not repairable inside it. [_Decomposition_](02-intent-into-items.md#decomposition) is the first: an item that should have been three names it, decomposition runs again over the intent, and the set that comes out is decided at Decomposition like any other. [_The interview_](02-intent-into-items.md#the-interview) is the second and the earliest thing reachable: a stage that cannot author because the request itself is underdetermined names the intent, which returns to the unrefined state an intent starts in and raises a question for whoever holds (3). That is where an ambiguity only the person who asked can settle belongs, and reaching it is the whole reason the floor is not Spec: re-authoring a spec four times against the same underdetermined request spends the limit and then asks that question anyway, one escalation later than needed.

The stage's agent can send an item back too, and that is where the defect is usually found first: the agent working from a spec is the one that discovers it says two things. It ends its attempt without authoring an artifact and names what owns the defect — a **rework request** — and no gate fires, there being no artifact for one to decide over. A human [backstopping](../what-humans-do.md) the stage (11, 12) sends one the same way, authorship being an attribute of the stage and the rework request being the stage's. It is written into the log by the log, with whoever was authoring as the actor, which is the arrangement the third kind of hold already takes for something that happened where no gate fired. A rework request is not a hold, which is the record it most resembles: it counts an attempt where a hold counts none, and where it names a stage it is an outcome on the author of that stage's artifact, which the [per-author prior](04-risk-score.md#factors-at-least) reads like every other outcome on that author's work. Naming decomposition or the intent moves no prior at all: decomposition proposes a set rather than an artifact, and an intent is not an artifact either — the person who asked is not its author in the sense the prior keeps, and moving theirs would score an owner for having asked a question the factory could not answer. A rework request is never a reject — no gate fired and no verdict was given — so no threshold learns from one either way.

The attempt is counted at what the item is sent to, and that is the rule for a reject naming a target as much as for a rework request: the rework is booked against the thing that was wrong. Against a stage that is the stage's own count on the item; against decomposition or the intent it is one of the two counts the intent keeps, its re-decompositions and its interview rounds, which is why neither target needs a counter of its own. The stages below the target then re-author counting nothing, the rule [_The merge queue_](05-environments.md#the-merge-queue) already keeps for candidates re-verifying behind someone else's failure: they are being redone because of somebody else, and a count that rose for that would escalate the stage that noticed the defect rather than the one that made it. So a spec returned to four times escalates at Spec (12), and that is what ends the loop: an item cannot go around the pipeline indefinitely, because every rework request costs an attempt at the stage it names. The cost of not counting below: an item can spend four implementations and four builds while Implementation's count stands at one, so what a stage cost is answerable from the spend [_What an item names_](02-intent-into-items.md#what-an-item-names) keeps beside the count and never from the count itself, and [_Factory_](11-screens.md#work-ops-factory-people)'s rework rate reports the stage that was wrong rather than the work that was redone.

What a rework request costs: an item's position stops being something anyone can recompute. Dispatch matches a stage against a [role](01-one-pipeline.md) and writes the transition it is told about, so a rework request is the one thing that moves an item where neither a match nor a gate decided it: an agent decides that its own item goes backwards, and nothing decides over that. Nothing but the count limits it, so an agent that sends back every spec it is given spends that item's attempts at Spec and turns work the factory could have finished into an escalation (12) a human clears. Naming the intent is the same failure aimed at a person: an agent that finds every request underdetermined turns the factory into a questionnaire, and the rounds limit it the way they limit an interview going in circles.

An item sent back to a stage re-authors every stage below it. Sent past its stages it re-authors what the answer or the re-decomposition actually changed and no more: an item the re-decomposition left standing resumes where it was, and one it replaced is superseded and authors nothing again. What was already authored is not removed — the versions stay in the chain the artifact store keeps, so a decision naming one still points at what was decided over — but nothing below the target stands as approved: a plan authored from a [spec version](#spec) since superseded is not this item's plan, and leaving it approved would have the [_Implementation_](#implementation) gate decide a build against it. The gates below fire again on what is re-authored. What that costs is everything spent below the target — a build among it where the item had got that far — the same cost that gate already carries for a rejection of its own.

A spec version authored on a rework request introduces and withdraws [acceptance criteria](#spec) like any other, and an item returning to Spec to drop a behaviour has to withdraw its criterion explicitly. A criterion is a record of the service, written once and never written again, so one introduced by the version being replaced stays in force until a version withdraws it, and leaving it out of the new version withdraws nothing.

A rework request to a stage ends nothing. The item keeps its [branch](05-environments.md#records-and-one-long-lived-branch) and its builds, which are freed for good when it merges, is dropped, or is superseded by a re-decomposition — the last of those being the one way a rework request ends an item rather than sending it back anywhere. Its [environment](05-environments.md#an-environment-per-candidate), where it has one, is reclaimed while the item is stopped above Implementation and composed again when it next reaches the candidate deploy gate, which [_An environment per candidate_](05-environments.md#an-environment-per-candidate) sets out. So an item sent from the Merge to master gate back to Spec pays a second composition when it returns, instead of holding an environment nothing is running on for as long as the spec, the plan, the tasks, and the implementation take.

## What particular gates decide

What each of the eight rows decides, taken one at a time, and then the two rows that are not among them.

### Decomposition

The one gate where approving admits several timelines at once. Everything below it is per item, and it fires only where decomposition yielded more than one. [_Decomposition_](02-intent-into-items.md#decomposition) sets out the stage, what is decided at it, and the drawback of firing it conditionally.

### Spec

One duty, and one confirmation already made. [_The interview_](02-intent-into-items.md#the-interview) (3) refines intent and the spec is what it produces; the person who asked confirmed the factory's reading of the request at the interview's last round, which no score gates, so there is no interview gate and none is missing. A spec version names the acceptance criteria it introduces and the ones it withdraws, so approving it confirms that change to the set (6). What is confirmed is the criteria; the encoding that decides them against an observed run is authored at [_Implementation_](#implementation) and is downstream of this approval, not the object of it.

A criterion is a record of the service and the spec does not restate it. A restated criterion would let the spec and the criterion disagree — the reason an item names no [project](11-screens.md#work-ops-factory-people) — and it would make ids stable and never reused a rule about nothing. The record is written exactly once, by the [artifact store](01-one-pipeline.md) in the same call that submits the spec version introducing it, and never written again: withdrawal is recorded on the withdrawing spec version, so a version the gate rejects takes its withdrawal down with it. Which criteria are in force for a build is then a query, and a build is a set of items — the ones merged into the repository it was made from, plus the item whose branch it is. A criterion is in force for a build where the item that introduced it is in that build and no spec version in that build withdraws it. That reading is right for master and for the withdrawing candidate at once, where a field written at either moment would be wrong for the other, and both halves have to read the same set: a criterion introduced by a sibling that has not merged is a promise this build's tree could not keep, so holding it in force would reject every candidate decomposed in parallel with the one that introduced it. What that costs is that the set in force differs between two candidates of one service at the same moment, so what a service promises is read from the build running in production and never from the criterion records alone. A human confirming (6) reads what the version changes and not the accumulated set; what a service promises now is the set in force for its current release, and [Factory](11-screens.md#work-ops-factory-people) reports how many are in force, how many were admitted as exceptions — sentences that fit no pattern — how many have never failed, and what share of them are unwanted conditions, so drift is visible rather than re-read. The cost: a set that drifted is never re-read unless an owner's safeguard puts a human here, and an item legitimately changing a behaviour has to withdraw the old criterion and remove its encoding in one item — the one edit that removes protection.

Criteria are predicates, not prose, for the reason a [consumer contract](07-contracts.md#what-a-consumer-declares) is: what cannot be decided against an observed run is checked by nobody, and in the steady state that is most of them — the factory drafts its own criteria and a human reads them only where the score or a safeguard (9) puts one there. Each states one testable behaviour in one of a closed set of six sentence patterns, drawn from EARS.

| Pattern | The sentence it makes |
|---|---|
| **always true** | `the system shall <response>` |
| **event** | `When <trigger>, the system shall <response>` |
| **state** | `While <state>, the system shall <response>` |
| **unwanted condition** | `If <condition>, then the system shall <response>` |
| **optional feature** | `Where <feature is included>, the system shall <response>` |
| **state with an event inside it** | `While <state>, when <trigger>, the system shall <response>` |

Ids are stable and never reused, the same rule the [release number](06-releases.md#the-release-number) keeps, and a criterion that is dropped stays withdrawn rather than freeing its id. The rule is the same and the mechanism is not: the release number is an ordinal because ordering builds is its job, and its position in master's linear history is what lets [_The merge queue_](05-environments.md#the-merge-queue) re-derive one after a stop or a restore from a backup, where a criterion id orders nothing and nothing outside the factory records it — so a criterion id is opaque, drawn so that minting cannot repeat one, and a restore that loses criterion records loses no counter a later mint would rewind. What opacity costs is an id a human reads nothing from, which the release number's whole job is to provide. A sentence fitting no pattern is admitted with a tagged reason and counted: a form everything can escape is not a form.

A criterion may state a quality of the behaviour as well as the behaviour — a latency, a property of the rendered screen — and the six patterns already carry one: the response clause holds the measurement, so `When the export is requested, the system shall respond within 200 ms under the stated load` is the event pattern like any other. What admits it is the rule that admits every criterion, decidability against an observed run, which for a quality criterion means its encoding produces the condition it measures on [the candidate environment](05-environments.md#what-the-candidate-environment-decides): load the encoding generates, stated in the criterion's sentence, or a render the encoding drives and reads. An encoding whose load is stated leaves no unloaded run to disagree with a loaded one, so it goes [undecided](05-environments.md#what-the-candidate-environment-decides) only the way any encoding does. What it establishes is bounded and named: behaviour on that environment's hardware under that load, which is not production's scale — production is read afterwards, by [the health monitor](08-operations.md#the-health-monitor)'s comparison and by [an explicit threshold](08-operations.md#the-health-monitor) a safeguard (9) sets, and [_Does the explicit health threshold link to what motivated it?_](../open.md#does-the-explicit-health-threshold-link-to-what-motivated-it) is the question that residue leaves open. What it costs is the most expensive encoding kind there is: generated load and a driven render spend candidate compute at every run, and an owner whose complaint was production's scale has bought a check that cannot see it.

The form is the whole of what this provides, and having it makes one failure worse: pattern-perfect criteria can describe an incomplete set, and the incompleteness is hidden behind a passing check. The unwanted conditions are where that happens — a set of only happy-path criteria is half a set, and nothing mechanical will ever ask for the other half.

What is available instead of a check is a count, which is why the share of criteria written in the unwanted-condition pattern is one of the numbers Factory reports. Nothing decides on it and no gate reads it: a set with none is visible without anything having judged that it should have some. The cost: the number means little against one item — an item genuinely has no unwanted condition sometimes — so it is worth reading across a service and over time rather than against a spec, and an agent that has learned it is counted can satisfy it with unwanted conditions nobody needed.

Where the item has a user interface, the spec version introduces a screen's **state machine** — the states a screen can be in, the events that move it between them, and what each state forbids, empty and loading and failed among them. It is part of what a human confirms here (6) and not one of the six patterns: the patterns are sentences and lose the transitions, and it is the transitions that make [_Implementation_](#implementation)'s rejection mechanical. It is a record of the service like a criterion, written by the artifact store in the same call, and the screen's identity is the id of the state machine that introduced it — per service, stable, never reused, the rule the criterion id and the release number both keep, and opaque by the criterion id's mechanism rather than the release number's. A revision is a new state machine naming the one it supersedes, and the chain of supersessions is the screen; there is no screen record, because a screen with no state machine is nothing the factory can check. The cost is that nothing mechanical stops two items introducing two chains for what a human would call one screen, and what catches that is the owner confirming here — the one place the flow's enforcement rests on a human noticing rather than on a link.

The drawback is the authoring, the same one declared meaning has in [_What a diff cannot see_](07-contracts.md#what-a-diff-cannot-see) — a sketch is cheaper and checks nothing. What the screen looks like is not here: that is the design system the project chose as a [permanent constraint](02-intent-into-items.md#constraints-and-the-design-system) (2), a screen design that arrived with the request and binds only what was decomposed from it, and, where neither answers, [_Implementation plan_](#implementation-plan).

### Implementation plan

How the item will be built, and the decisions no permanent constraint (2) answers — what a screen looks like where the design system is silent is the common one. That is what Edit in place is for: the plan is a document, so a human who wants a different approach edits one into it rather than rejecting the item to get one. Reject is for a plan that is wrong, not for one that is merely not theirs.

### Tasks

A task is an internal step of one item and never a unit that ships: one item is one release, and a task has no build, no number, and no environment of its own. The factory authors them from the approved plan — the plan is how the item will be built, the tasks are that divided into work an agent picks up — and they are complete when the implementation is.

What the gate provides is a look at the breakdown before agents work on it, and Edit in place is where a human resequences or splits one without changing the plan above it. A task that cannot be finished escalates nothing by itself: the attempt limit is per stage, so what appears in Work (12) is the item.

### Implementation

Where the state machine the spec authored is enforced. An implementation that admits a forbidden transition is rejected here, mechanically rather than on taste — the whole reason for authoring the screen as a state machine. This is also the first gate that decides over a build: the build is made from the item's candidate branch when the stage finishes, so a build that does not compile is rejected here where Reject with feedback is an action, and the score computes the [change factors](04-risk-score.md#factors-at-least) from that build's diff against master. The drawback: an item rejected here has already spent a build.

It is also the one artifact gate with no Edit in place, for the reason [_What a gate may change_](#what-a-gate-may-change) gives. The actions are the narrowest in the table: approve what was built, or [send the item back](#going-back-up) — to this stage to have it built differently, or to the stage whose artifact was the thing that was wrong. What the human reads is the build's diff against master, rendered in [Work](11-screens.md#work-ops-factory-people) — the one place the product renders code, the exception [_What the factory does not build_](../what-the-factory-does.md#what-the-factory-does-not-build) states — beside the list of what the change reaches that the [vector](04-risk-score.md#factors-at-least)'s exposure factor carries. The artifact under decision is the implementation, and a list derived from it is not it: a gate that showed the list and withheld the code would record a human decision over material no human read.

The encoding of the acceptance criteria is authored here as well. A criterion is a predicate and something has to decide it against an observed run — unit tests are today's form of that — so the encoding is written with the code it checks and is reviewed at this gate as an artifact of the item. An artifact of the item is not always a record: the encoding is code in the build, picked out by the criterion id it names and derived from the build the way a consumer contract is, so it cannot outlive the criterion it was written for. It runs where there is a run to observe, on [_the candidate environment_](05-environments.md#what-the-candidate-environment-decides), and what it produces attaches to the build and is read at [_Merge to master_](#merge-to-master). This gate rejects in both directions: a criterion in force for the build with no encoding naming it, and an encoding naming a criterion that same build withdraws — otherwise the item that withdraws a criterion leaves its encoding in master, deciding a promise the service no longer makes. What the [health monitor](08-operations.md#the-health-monitor) will read is checked the same way and at the same gate. The numbers that comparison reads are emitted by the software the factory wrote, so a build emitting none of them is a build whose [analysis window](08-operations.md#the-analysis-window) measures nothing while reporting that it watched — the reading [_The analysis window_](08-operations.md#the-analysis-window) already refuses for an absent input, arriving one gate earlier. So the emission is an artifact of the item like the encoding is, derived from the build, and this gate rejects in both directions over it too: a service whose build stops emitting a number its windows were being decided on, and an emission naming a number nothing reads. What that costs: a change to what a service measures is a change this gate can stop, a stop on work that is often the point of the item.

Its authority comes from the gate rather than from the encoding being faithful, the same arrangement [_What a consumer declares_](07-contracts.md#what-a-consumer-declares) uses for an artifact the factory derived. The drawback: an agent that misread a criterion can encode its misreading and pass what it wrote. What limits that is the criterion a human confirmed (6) one gate up: it is unchanged, it is still the thing being checked, and an encoding that departs from it is rejected here like any other artifact that does not do what it was authored from.

That limit is weaker than it reads, because in the steady state the score auto-passes the Spec gate — an intent grouped from [reports](02-intent-into-items.md#reports) excepted, its source resolving a human to that gate — and the criterion was confirmed by nobody. So one rule governs the authoring: **an encoding's expected behaviour is derived from the criterion's sentence, never from the code it checks.** The two are authored in the same stage by the same agent, and an encoding written by reading an implementation cannot disagree with that implementation — a defect in the code becomes the expected result, and the run that decides the criterion then passes on it. Reading the criterion first is the only independence available, the sentence being the one thing that existed before the code.

It is an instruction to the agent and not a check, and it is worth what an instruction is worth. What would make it mechanical is a decision procedure whose ground truth comes from somewhere other than the agent that wrote the code, and the shapes that exist are few — a generator working from the criterion, a set of cases a human approved, an invariant asserted over real data, or mutation of the code to see whether the encoding notices. Each costs authoring this stage does not do today, and none is chosen here. Until one is, the honest statement is that a passing criterion establishes that an encoding of it passes, which is not the same as the criterion being met, and the gate rather than the encoding is what the factory rests on.

### Deploy to candidate environment

The dependency its hold waits on was declared at decomposition, so what this gate waits for is mostly known before the item was ever built. Here the wait is for that dependency to become live at all, since the environment is composed from it. The same dependency is checked once more at the production deploy gate, where the question is whether it is live still. One condition here decomposition could not have declared: a platform with no room for another environment, which holds rather than rejects — a rejection would count an attempt and teach the score something the item did not do — and lifts when an environment is freed: an item merging, dropped, or superseded, or one [reclaimed](05-environments.md#an-environment-per-candidate) from an item running nothing. What that costs: an item stopped for a reason no artifact of it explains and no parameter of an owner's limits — the factory's own infrastructure ceiling, visible only as a wait.

Nothing else attaches to this row — no strategy, no rollout, no analysis window — for the reason [_What the candidate environment decides_](05-environments.md#what-the-candidate-environment-decides) gives. What the deploy provides is the criteria.

### Merge to master

The release event — where a candidate becomes a numbered release, and where the verdict on the candidate is given. Approving it admits the candidate to [the merge queue](05-environments.md#the-merge-queue), which re-verifies it against the master it will actually merge into and rejects it there on the same terms if that fails; rejecting it here [sends the item back](#going-back-up) to the stage the verdict names and to Implementation where it names none, and nothing waits on the environment it used, which stays the item's until it merges, is dropped, or is superseded by a re-decomposition. The verdict is a human's when the score or a safeguard puts one there and the factory's own otherwise, and a human deciding there is performing [UAT](../what-humans-do.md) (7). What either reads is the candidate's own run: the acceptance criteria decided against it, every consumer contract checked against it, and the producer's own contract diff — each rejecting on its own terms before anyone gives a verdict.

### Deploy to production

The default path's exception to reject, which is why that row does not offer it. By the time a change reaches it, the merge has happened and the release number is already assigned, so hold is the only way to stop it. Once it deploys, all that is left is a human's undo of a shipped change (10) — a rollback while its control is still running, a revert after, which is a new item.

Its hold also fires on the factory's own account, like the one a rollback leaves in place. A dependency declared at decomposition that is not its service's [_current release_](06-releases.md#the-deploy-record) when this gate fires holds the deploy — current meaning marked complete on every production target, so a producer deployed to three targets of four holds its consumer until the fourth lands — and the check runs at the moment of firing — the same rule the [two contract baselines](07-contracts.md#what-a-consumer-declares) keep — because a producer that was live when its consumer verified can be rolled back before that consumer deploys. Nothing is decided and no [_page_](08-operations.md#pages) fires; the hold lifts when the dependency is current again. A human can approve through it, as through every hold the factory sets, and what approving accepts is the break the hold was preventing. An [error budget](08-operations.md#service-level-objectives) exhausted holds the deploy the same way and lifts itself the same way, and two items pass it rather than waiting: a revert, and an item the health monitor raised on that service.

The hold [_Drift detection_](08-operations.md#drift-detection) sets is the other kind and the only one of it: what the factory recorded about this service is not what is running, so nothing here can be decided on the record, and no evidence the factory can gather lifts it. That one pages, for the reason the dependency hold does not — it waits on a human and on nothing else. Approving through is still offered, and here it is the human saying the record is wrong and the deploy should proceed anyway.

What it does not cover is the consumer already in production when its producer rolls back. A deploy gate stops deploys, and by then there is nothing left to stop — what catches that one is the consumer's own error rate raising an item, the same answer the factory gives to every consumer break it cannot see from the producer's side.

### A role prompt or a skill

The first of two rows outside the default path and outside every item. What it decides is a version of what an agent is told — a [_role prompt_](10-fleet.md#what-an-agent-is-told) or a [_skill_](10-fleet.md#what-an-agent-is-told), authored by an agent from the factory's own decisions, or entered unauthored at an upgrade's first start — the one exception [_One pipeline_](01-one-pipeline.md) states, install's entries alone firing none — and written by the [artifact store](01-one-pipeline.md). Its actions are Approve, Reject with feedback, and Edit in place, which is what every authoring row offers, and for the same reason: what is under decision is a document, and a human who wants different words writes them.

There is no Hold, because a Hold would name a state the row is already in: a version not approved simply is not in force, and the one below it goes on being in force — or, for a chain an upgrade started, nothing is, and only the work that would have used it waits — where every other gate's hold exists to stop an event that would otherwise happen.

What it reads is the factor set [_Factors, at least_](04-risk-score.md#factors-at-least) names for a version, and its threshold is a field of the [factory-wide settings record](09-gate-policy.md#one-shape-across-all-of-them) rather than of an [environment](05-environments.md#records-and-one-long-lived-branch): there is no project here to have a production environment, and the row exists before any project does. A [safeguard](09-gate-policy.md#one-shape-across-all-of-them) (9) reaches it on subjects a safeguard may already name and no new kind: a [stage](01-one-pipeline.md) for a role prompt, a role prompt being per role and most roles naming the work of one stage — the [report store](02-intent-into-items.md#reports) for [the grouper](02-intent-into-items.md#reports)'s, its role being no stage's — and the [area](../what-humans-do.md), [service](02-intent-into-items.md#decomposition), or [project](11-screens.md#work-ops-factory-people) a skill is bound to for a skill. So an owner who has safeguarded an area already gets a human at every skill version written for it, without having asked for that twice.

Reject sends nothing back, there being no stage above this row to send it to, so [_Going back up_](#going-back-up) does not apply. What a reject leaves is a version nobody approved and the feedback that says why, which the next version is authored from — so [_the attempt limit_](#the-attempt-limit) does not reach this row — it counts a stage's attempts on an item, and there is neither. A factory that keeps authoring a version nobody approves keeps authoring it, and this is the one loop the limit does not close. What ends it is the evidence going away or an owner leaving the row alone, neither of which is a mechanism.

### A safeguard's withdrawal

The second row outside every item. What it decides is the record [_A safeguard is not edited_](09-gate-policy.md#one-shape-across-all-of-them) makes a withdrawal — a second record naming the [safeguard](09-gate-policy.md#one-shape-across-all-of-them) (9) it ends — and a withdrawal is not in force until this row approves it: the value in force reads the newest approved record for that subject, and a withdrawal nobody approved leaves the safeguard standing. It has a row because it is the one operation in the factory that removes a human from a gate, which the document refuses to any mechanism; a record that did it with no decision on it would be that mechanism with an actor field for a conscience. Its actions are Approve and Reject with feedback: there is no Edit in place, a withdrawal naming one record having nothing in it to edit, and no Hold, for the reason the row above gives — a withdrawal not approved is already not in force.

It reads no factor set and no threshold. A human is at it always, the way a resolved factor puts one at a gate whatever the number — the [score](04-risk-score.md) deciding to remove a human is what [_How it learns_](04-risk-score.md#how-it-learns) refuses, and a row the score could auto-pass would be the refusal with a gap in it. Who that human is follows the safeguard's own routing field: the [duty](../what-humans-do.md) or named human its rows route to, and the owner where it names none — and never the actor on the withdrawal, so the person who ends a check is not the person who decides it ends. Where those are one person, the row still fires and the decision is still written: an install with one owner cannot separate the two, and what the row buys there is a chained [decision](#where-a-gate-is-and-what-decides-it) with an actor and a time on it rather than a second pair of eyes, which is what an auditor reads two months later. Reject leaves the safeguard in force and the feedback beside the withdrawal, and [_the attempt limit_](#the-attempt-limit) does not reach this row, there being no stage and no item. What it costs: every withdrawal waits on a human, a safeguard whose named human has left holds until the owner clears it — the widening an unheld row already gets — and the actor on both records is self-asserted until [seam 1](../deferred.md) carries a principal, so what the row records is who claimed to decide, still one more thing than a field flipped in place.
