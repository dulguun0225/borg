# Dispatch

With the decomposition approved, the factory puts its own agents onto the items — a model in a role with a scope, as [_One pipeline_](../01-one-pipeline.md) has it. Dispatch gets no gate: it decides who authors, and everything authored is reviewed at a gate anyway, where the score reads the [per-author prior](../04-risk-score/01-factors-at-least.md) of the model that wrote it.

What limits how many items move at once is infrastructure — an environment per candidate — and that cost is the factory's rather than a human's. Where a human authors a stage instead of an agent (11, 12), nothing about dispatch changes: authorship is an attribute of the stage, and the item is still one timeline.

Dispatch is report-driven — [_What an item names_](03-decomposition/02-what-an-item-names.md) has every stage reporting a transition to it — and an agent can stop without reporting anything: its host restarts, its process dies, its provider connection hangs. So a dispatch is a claim that expires unless the agent renews it, renewal being activity rather than progress, and a claim that expires unrenewed is itself the report: dispatch sends the item back to the stage to be entered again, the way an agent's failed attempt does, so the count rises on re-entry and enough silent stops walk the item to an [escalation](../03-gates/05-the-attempt-limit.md) (12) instead of leaving a timeline that never advances and never escalates. A stage with an unexpired claim is matched to nobody, which is what keeps a slow agent and its replacement from authoring one stage at once. What it costs is a renewal on every working agent, and that a stop is discovered at the expiry rather than at the death — an expiry generous enough to never cut off honest work leaves a dead stage undiscovered for exactly that long.

The [_Factory_](../11-screens.md#work-ops-factory-people) screen is where an owner watches this: [_the fleet_](../10-fleet/README.md), what each agent is doing, and how well.
