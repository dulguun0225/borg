# Contracts

What binds one service to another and to its own past, and what stops a change breaking either.

## Two versioned things

A [project](11-screens.md#work-ops-factory-people)'s software is one or more services, and services reach each other through published interfaces — REST under OpenAPI, gRPC under protobuf, Kafka under topic schemas. Those interfaces have consumers, and the consumers are other services in the same factory, whichever project each is in. Nothing here permits or forbids one service calling another: code calls what it calls, and the factory is not in the network path. What the reach decides is what the factory checks and records — the version, the [consumer contract](#what-a-consumer-declares) checked at the producer's [Merge to master](03-gates.md#merge-to-master) gate, the [deprecation list](#deprecation), the producer's [current release](06-releases.md#the-deploy-record) running in the consumer's [candidate environment](05-environments.md#an-environment-per-candidate). A call it does not cover still happens and breaks the way a third-party interface breaks. What covering a cross-project call costs is that the [items](01-one-pipeline.md) decomposed from one [intent](02-intent-into-items.md#intake) are decided under two projects' thresholds and [safeguards](09-gate-policy.md#one-shape-across-all-of-them), and that a [fleet](10-fleet.md) scoped to one project cannot be dispatched onto the item in the other.

A contract is a record: one published interface of one service, identified by that service and the interface's own name in its build, with its kind — a published interface or a [store](#the-store-is-a-contract-too) — as the field everything else follows from. [The merge queue](05-environments.md#the-merge-queue) writes it at the fast-forward of the first release that publishes it, in the same write as that release's first version, because a contract changes only inside its service's items and every write to it happens at a release. A [writer](../what-the-factory-does.md#tight-integration) of its own would be a component with one caller and the per-service ordering implemented again inside it, which is the argument that already put the [release record](06-releases.md#the-release-record) and its number in the queue. The kind has to be single-valued across versions, which is why it is a record and not a name for a service plus an interface name: two versions disagreeing about whether the thing is a store would enforce two promises on one interface. What it costs is that a contract exists only from the merge that first published it, so an interface a rejected [candidate](06-releases.md#what-a-build-is-called-and-when) publishes points at nothing.

So there are two versioned things and they must not be combined into one. A release number orders the builds of a single service. A contract version is a compatibility promise to whoever calls it, and semver is precisely what that is for: major means a consumer breaks. One service can publish several contracts — an API, a gRPC service, three topics — and they move at their own rates. A single number per service cannot express several independent promises, and a promise that moves for unrelated reasons stops being read. Most releases publish no new contract version at all.

## No single item may break a contract

**No single item may break a contract.** A breaking change is four items: the producer adds the new **form** — what the interface's build declares, its fields, operations or messages and their types — beside the old, each consumer migrates, the producer runs a [brownout](#deprecation) of the old form, and the producer removes it. Each ships alone, is verified on an environment of its own, and is independently reversible. This is the same discipline [no-batching](06-releases.md#one-item-per-release) already forces — an item that cannot ship by itself was [decomposed](02-intent-into-items.md#decomposition) wrong — and the two rules support each other. Where a change genuinely cannot be decomposed, that is an [escalation](03-gates.md#the-attempt-limit) (12), not permission to batch — and taking it over gives a human no power the factory lacks, since a human authoring a [stage](01-one-pipeline.md) instead of an [agent](01-one-pipeline.md) changes the author and not the stages, the gates, or this rule. What the escalation asks for is a re-decomposition, not a co-deploy built by hand — and a stage that finds it [names decomposition](03-gates.md#going-back-up) rather than escalating, a re-decomposition being something the factory performs on an intent in progress and not only a thing to ask a human for.

The four items are also where the version moves, and it moves twice: the addition is a minor, each consumer's migration leaves it where it is — so does the brownout, which changes behaviour and not the form — and the removal is the major. Inside the factory that major warns nobody — the removal was raised only after the list [_Deprecation_](#deprecation) keeps had emptied and the brownout had run uncrossed, so every consumer the factory can read had already migrated — and what it records is that the old form is gone. Its promise is kept for the consumer the factory cannot read, which is the one case where major means what semver says it does.

## What a contract promises

Every contract promises **backward** compatibility — the new build reads what the old one wrote — which is what a consumer of an interface needs. A store promises **forward** as well, and over writes as well as reads: the build being restored reads what the newer one wrote, and the schema the newer one installed still accepts what the restored build writes. Both are enforced on every diff after that.

Nothing is declared. Which promise a contract makes follows from whether it is a store, which the factory already knows, and there is no third case: forward alone has no user, because anything a rollback reads is read going the other way too. A declared mode would be three values where the kind of the thing decides among them, and each of the three is one more thing every builder implements identically.

The drawback is that a derived promise is assumed where a declared one is checked. A producer that wants more than its kind provides has nowhere to say so, and the factory's answer to a wrong derivation is that there is nothing to derive — the kind is a fact about the contract and not a judgment about it.

## Enforcement

The rule is mechanical rather than a judgment call. The factory diffs the contract a candidate publishes against the one in production: a diff the contract's promise allows passes, and a breaking diff without the migration already shipped ahead of it is a rejection at the Merge to master gate. The [same graph](../what-the-factory-does.md#tight-integration) answers who is affected — the factory knows which services consume which contracts, so "what does this break" is a query rather than an estimate, and it feeds the [context factor](04-risk-score.md#factors-at-least) of the risk score directly.

## What a diff cannot see

A diff proves the shape is unchanged, not that callers still work. A field that quietly stops being populated, or that keeps its name and its type and changes its units, breaks a consumer without breaking a contract. Neither promise catches it: both are checked against the schema, and the damage is in the semantics. Three layers answer it, and not one of them is a recording.

**Meaning is declared, so the diff can see it.** A unit belongs to a field's identity rather than to a note about it — `send_time_millis`, not `send_time` — and so does whether the field is always populated. A change of units is then a rename, a rename is a breaking diff, and the four items of a migration handle it with nothing new. The drawback is the authoring: the factory holds a contract to the consumer contract when it is written, an interface adopted from outside the factory arrives without it, and retrofitting the name is itself a breaking change — four items to rename a field no behaviour depends on. It covers what someone thought to declare and nothing else.

**The producer compares its own observables.** Per field, on the producer's own traffic: population rate, the spread of enum values, the distribution of numbers, each against the release this one replaced. A field populated 99.9% of the time for a year that drops to 80% is invisible to a schema check and obvious in a profile, and a unit swapped for one a thousand times finer moves every number by a thousand. What it does with a finding is raise an item and feed the context factor of the risk score; it rejects nothing, because a statistical call is arguable where a schema diff is not, and enforcement stays mechanical by not admitting one. Every number in it is the producer's own, so nothing has to be recorded or attributed across services. The one exemption is a marked element whose [brownout](#deprecation) is running: its numbers dropping is the change itself, made on purpose, and a finding raised on it would be an item no code change resolves. An owner who wants explicit thresholds adds them with a safeguard (9).

**A consumer's assumptions are checked against the candidate.** The factory already knows who consumes what, so the candidate running on its own environment is checked against every consumer contract, and a failure is a rejection at the Merge to master gate like any breaking diff. No [window](08-operations.md#the-analysis-window) limits it — a consumer called once a month is covered exactly as well as one on a hot path. A [consumer contract's predicate](#what-a-consumer-declares) — one assertion the factory can decide against a single observed exchange — has a third outcome for the same reason a [criterion](03-gates.md#spec) does: where the candidate's run produced no exchange to decide it against, it is **undecided** rather than passing, and undecided is read at the Merge to master gate the way a failure is, which is the treatment [_What the candidate environment decides_](05-environments.md#what-the-candidate-environment-decides) already sets out. A predicate decided against nothing passes every check the factory has and assures nothing, and the consumer whose path a run never exercised is exactly the consumer the check exists for — so counting an unexercised predicate as a pass would make the coverage this paragraph claims a coverage of whatever the producer's own run happened to touch. What it costs is that a consumer on a path the run cannot reach holds its producer's merge until the run reaches it, which is a stop where the alternative is a promise nothing tested. [_What a consumer declares_](#what-a-consumer-declares) sets out what may be declared, where a consumer contract comes from, and what it requires.

Replaying each consumer's recorded actual usage is the layer that is not built, and not for its size. What a recording proves is limited to what ran inside its retention window, so the expensive option is also the one with the gap: a consumer on a monthly path falls outside any window worth keeping. A consumer contract gives the same protection without the recording.

What none of the three catches is a semantic change nobody declared and no distribution shift reveals. What catches that is the consumer breaking in its own error rate and raising an item there, the criteria confirmed at the [Spec gate](03-gates.md#spec), and [a human undoing the change after it shipped](../what-humans-do.md) (10) while it can still be undone.

## What a consumer declares

**A consumer contract is a predicate, not prose.** It is contract testing's consumer-driven contract — what a consumer assumes of a producer, as against the [contract](#two-versioned-things) the producer publishes — and it has to be decidable by the factory against one observed exchange, and it is drawn from the list of allowed predicate kinds the factory owns: the field is read at all, it arrives populated, it has this unit, its values stay inside this domain or this range. The list grows with the rest of [gate policy](09-gate-policy.md) (8); a consumer picks from it and cannot invent a kind of assertion when one is derived. An assumption that will not fit a predicate is not in a consumer contract at all — it stays the consumer's own acceptance criteria, and an item against the producer if it is ever violated.

**It is derived from the consumer's build, not entered by hand.** A **consumer contract** is a record and an artifact of the consumer's item, written through the [artifact store](01-one-pipeline.md) at the [implementation stage](03-gates.md#implementation) from that item's build — by whoever authors the stage, an agent or a human backstopping it, and derived by the factory either way rather than typed. It has no field naming the release it entered force at: no release exists when it is written, and the merge queue writing one later would be a second writer. Which release a consumer contract entered force at is reached through the release record naming the same item — the release names the merge queue's [re-verification](05-environments.md#the-merge-queue) build where the consumer contract names the implementation stage's, and [_One item per release_](06-releases.md#one-item-per-release) keeps that join single-valued — so a consumer contract derived for a candidate that never merges is filtered by there being no release naming its item. A service derives one against its own store contract exactly as against another service's interface, which is what makes the store's consumer — the service's own past — readable by the same query and lets a deprecated column's list empty. The factory reads the consumer and derives what it assumes, again at every release, so a consumer contract cannot outlive the code that motivated it.

Which consumer contracts bind at a moment is a query: for one service, those derived from every release from its [_last known-good release_](08-operations.md#overlapping-windows) — what a rollback could return to at all — up to its newest release. A service none of whose windows has closed [passed](08-operations.md#the-analysis-window) or timed out has no last known-good release, and the range is then every release it has, which is the direction a first release's missing [rollback target](08-operations.md#overlapping-windows) already takes. One range answers what runs now, what has merged and will run, and what a rollback can still restore, and it is the same query for an interface and for a store. The cost is that a removal waits until every consumer's migration release is that consumer's last known-good release, so on a quiet service the last step of a migration waits out the window's cap, and that the range is as wide as the windows a service has open and wider wherever a release has merged and not deployed, so a producer is held to the assumptions of releases that are not running. A consumer that stops reading a field stops declaring it: the list [_Deprecation_](#deprecation) waits on shortens itself, and its emptying raises the brownout alone — the removal follows only on the brownout's own evidence, the way [_Deprecation_](#deprecation) sets out — with nobody having to remember either. A consumer with no items for a year declares what its last release read, which is right — its code has not changed, so neither have its assumptions.

**Its authority comes from the gate, not from the derivation being right.** A derived consumer contract is an artifact of the consumer's item, ratified at the gates that item already passes — factory-authored, scored like anything else, read by a human where a safeguard (9) puts one there. Ratified that way it can reject a producer's candidate; before that it rejects nothing, which is true of every artifact here. A rejection later found wrong is a bad decision the score learns from, like a change a human undid after it shipped (10).

**Two baselines, kept apart.** A producer's own diff runs against the contract version its current release publishes, because the promise is to what is running. A consumer contract is checked against the contract version its producer's newest release publishes, because that is what the consumer will meet. Neither is written down and neither produces a record: both are computed at the moment the gate fires, and again inside the merge queue at re-verification, which rejects on the same terms with itself as the actor. So the race between the two resolves either way round it happens: a consumer that newly declares a field the producer is part-way through removing fails at its own gate, or the producer's removal candidate fails at its. What that costs is that a candidate which waited in the queue can fail on a baseline that moved after its own run passed, with no record of the earlier pass to point at. One gap is left rather than caught: a candidate whose form breaks against a merged but undeployed release of its own service passes the production diff, and minting its version against the newest one records the break as a major without rejecting anything — what catches it, where anything does, is a consumer contract naming the **element** — one field, one operation, or one message of the form.

**Where derivation cannot see, an owner supplies the predicate with a safeguard.** How much of a consumer's reading is visible is a property of that interface's toolchain rather than of the factory — a generated stub turns a field read into an accessor call, hand-parsed access hides it. A read the derivation misses is an unprotected assumption; one it invents holds a field until the next derivation drops it or the producer's blocked removal item asks the consumer to confirm. A safeguard is authored and maintained by an owner, so the blind case does not go unnoticed. Deriving at all assumes the consumer's build is the factory's to read, which [_Two versioned things_](#two-versioned-things) settles: a consumer outside the factory is blind for a harder reason — no build to read at all — so declared meaning and the producer's own observables are all that cover it, with a safeguard's predicate replacing the consumer contract.

**A derivation that could not run records that it could not, and the record resolves toward the human.** The derivation is per toolchain, so an extractor is a component of the factory, shipped with it, and which toolchains have one is published as a fact of the factory's version — readable before a service is adopted rather than discovered at its first removal. Where no extractor covers a consumer's build, or extraction fails outright, the outcome is **could not derive**: a record, not an empty list, because "no consumer reads this" and "no consumer's read was visible" call for opposite responses and a record that cannot tell them apart licenses the wrong one silently. What the record does is nothing new. The [context factor](04-risk-score.md#factors-at-least) reads which services consume what a candidate publishes; a consumer nobody could read makes that unknowable rather than zero, and [a factor the score cannot compute](04-risk-score.md#factors-at-least) already resolves to the value that puts a human at the gate, the [vector the decision records](04-risk-score.md#factors-at-least) naming the consumer nobody could read. Nothing bounds what an unreadable consumer consumes, so that reaches every candidate touching a published contract in the install, and a marked element's [deprecation list](#deprecation) can no longer be known empty, so no removal ships mechanically while the record stands. The cost is deliberate: one unsupported toolchain puts a human at contract-touching gates across the whole factory, which is standing, visible pressure to add the extractor or retire the service — where the silent reading, a consumer that could not be read scored as one that assumes nothing, made the changes most likely to break it the ones most likely to auto-pass.

## Who owns a contract

A contract belongs to the service that publishes it, and changes only inside that service's items. It gets no timeline of its own: a promise is only real when something serves it, so the authority to accept a change to one belongs where there is a build to check it against.

What a consumer gets is the promise its producer's kind of contract makes, and what it owns is the assumptions its own build declares — not a decision at every gate the producer passes. A consumer contract checked mechanically is not a veto — what answers it is the diff, not a person. The alternative was a per-change consumer veto, and it deadlocks — in a graph where nearly every service is somebody's consumer, items wait on each other until the [attempt limit](03-gates.md#the-attempt-limit) turns them into escalations (12), which is the human work the factory exists to remove. An objection raised after the fact is an item against the producer, joined to the original by intent, like any bug, and an owner who wants a human on a particular contract gets one with a safeguard (9).

## Deprecation

Deprecation is an obligation of the contract, not a note someone leaves. When the producer adds the new form, the old one is **marked** — a field of the contract version, per element, derived from the build like the rest of the form, because no diff shows which new form supersedes which old one and the producer's own item is the only thing that knows. Where a toolchain has no place to state it, a store schema being the ordinary case, the build states the mark beside the migration in the same file the derivation already reads. A mark is itself a change to the form, so it mints a version and it is a minor: a consumer sees a bump for an annotation nothing breaks on, which is the cost of the version tracking the form rather than the promise.

The list is then a query and nothing writes it — for one marked element, the consumers whose contracts in force still name it, plus any safeguard's predicate naming it. Computed, it cannot go stale: a consumer that stops reading a field leaves the list because its next release derives nothing, with nobody remembering to remove it. A maintained list would need two writers to agree about a fact both read out of records that already say it. A [_detector_](02-intent-into-items.md#intake) raises the brownout when the derived consumer contracts naming the element are gone — never again for an element whose brownout failed, the failed window standing in the record for the detector to read, both raises being a human's from then on — and the removal when the brownout's window has reached its cap uncrossed having received volume. Each raise is keyed by the contract and the element, the [evidence key intake states](02-intent-into-items.md#intake) for these raises, and the key is what stops a second brownout arriving beside one already raised. A safeguard stops neither item existing, only passing: the candidate is rejected at the Merge to master gate on the safeguard, counts an attempt, and appears in [Work](11-screens.md#work-ops-factory-people) as an escalation (12) naming the safeguard and its author, which is the blocked item asking the consumer to confirm. Nobody has to remember step three. What a stale safeguard costs is a full pass of [the pipeline](01-one-pipeline.md) and the attempt limit spent on work the factory raised itself, and a removal blocked for as long as nobody withdraws or confirms it.

The **brownout** is why the emptied list raises no removal by itself. The list is derived from builds, and [_What a consumer declares_](#what-a-consumer-declares) concedes what a build can hide: a read through reflection or configuration empties a list its consumer is still on. So the last inference before something is destroyed is checked against the running world instead of trusted. The brownout is an item of the producer whose release disables the marked element in behaviour while leaving the form unchanged — the field unpopulated, the operation erroring, deliberately the change [_What a diff cannot see_](#what-a-diff-cannot-see) names, made this once on purpose — and it ships, deploys, and rolls back like any release, under whatever [rollout strategy](03-gates.md#the-rollout-strategy) the score and an owner's safeguards pick; a read that lands on a [control](08-operations.md#the-health-monitor)'s instances errs nothing, which thins the signal the way partial exposure thins any window's. Its [window](08-operations.md#the-analysis-window) is the one window that reads more than the producer's own numbers — the exception is stated beside the rule it excepts, in [_The analysis window_](08-operations.md#the-analysis-window) — and it runs to the [cap](08-operations.md#the-analysis-window) rather than stopping where the [boundary](08-operations.md#the-analysis-window) would allow, the way the [held-out sample](04-risk-score.md#how-it-learns)'s does, its evidence being the point. A service breaking anywhere in the install while it is open fails it, and the failed exit is the ordinary one: the [rollback](06-releases.md#rollback) restores the element unattended, the [revert](06-releases.md#rollback) and the [hold](08-operations.md#overlapping-windows) follow as after any failed window, and the failed brownout stands in the record as a consumer read the derivation missed, so raising that element's brownout or removal again is from then on a human's act, never the detector's. The detector raises the removal only on a brownout window that reached the cap uncrossed having received volume — one whose comparison received none says nothing about the element, the reading [_The analysis window_](08-operations.md#the-analysis-window) gives an empty comparison — and that removal ships mechanically, unless a [could not derive](#what-a-consumer-declares) record stands, in which case the human [_What a consumer declares_](#what-a-consumer-declares) already puts at the gate is who decides it. The exit that reports no outcome is elsewhere not evidence; here the silence is the finding sought, because the brownout exists to give it a bounded period and a watch, and what it establishes stays bounded too: no read was exercised, never that none exists.

The brownout is the one release whose intended effect is a caller's failure — the factory degrading production on purpose, which nothing else may do — and the exception is stated the way [the search](08-operations.md#overlapping-windows)'s is, named and bounded: it ships as an item through every gate, one window bounds it, the ordinary rollback reverses it, and the removal it precedes would fail the same consumer permanently, with nothing watching and nothing left to restore. What it cannot establish is a read the window never saw — a consumer whose path runs monthly survives a shorter brownout and breaks at the removal — so it narrows what the blind case leaves unprotected and does not close it, and both levers over the rest are already an owner's: the cap sizes the period, and a safeguard (9) naming the element blocks the removal outright for the consumer an owner knows about. A false failure fails safe — a service erroring for reasons of its own restores an element nobody read and blocks a removal that was fine, at the cost any failed window carries, and the human who re-raises the removal is who tells the two apart. Brownouts hold windows open, so the [window limit](08-operations.md#overlapping-windows) already bounds how many elements a producer clears at once — and at the supplied limit of 1 a brownout is the producer's whole window budget for a full cap, its other releases holding at the production deploy gate meanwhile, the price the [held-out sample](04-risk-score.md#how-it-learns) already pays for the same run-to-cap watch. A store's old form takes no brownout: its consumer is the service's own past release, which is not running to err, and the release range [_What a consumer declares_](#what-a-consumer-declares) queries is already what covers it.

The list contains the consumers the factory can derive, so a consumer outside the factory is on it only where an owner's safeguard supplies a predicate replacing that consumer's contract — the blind case [_What a consumer declares_](#what-a-consumer-declares) sets out, and blocking the removal item is what the safeguard does here. Without one, it is not on the list at all: the list empties while that consumer still reads the old form, and the factory proceeds to the brownout and the removal on schedule. The brownout reaches it as a warning where the major version cannot — behavioural, needing no build to read — but nothing watches it: an outside consumer's error rate is not the factory's to read, so its break during the brownout restores nothing by itself, and what the brownout gives it is a bounded period in which the element still exists to be restored and a human to complain to, rather than a removal nothing can put back.

## The store is a contract too

A service's store is a contract too, and its consumer is the service's own past — the release that was running a minute ago, which a rollback can restore. That consumer is why a store promises **forward** as well: every rollback reads across the change in the direction backward alone does not cover, and it is the only kind of contract with a consumer of that kind. It is also a consumer that writes: a rollback makes the restored build the store's writer again, so its consumer contract is derived from writes as well as reads — the elements the code writes, and whether each is written populated and inside its domain, from the build like everything else — where an interface's consumer declares reads alone. The [declarations in force](#what-a-consumer-declares) are the same range query, from the last known-good release up to the newest, which is exactly the set of releases a rollback can put back in front of the store.

The rule is the same there: **no single item may break the store.** A breaking schema change is three items — the store gains the new form beside the old, the code migrates onto it, the old form is dropped — and while it remains, the old form has the same deprecation obligation an old interface has. The schema change itself is a step of the deploy that ships it, applied by the [deployer](08-operations.md#the-health-monitor) and named on the [deploy record](06-releases.md#the-deploy-record), and a rollback undoes none: the schema moves only forward, which is what makes this promise the one a rollback rests on.

Enforcement requires nothing new: the factory diffs the schema a candidate declares against the one in production, and a diff the declarations in force forbid without the migration already shipped ahead of it is a rejection at the Merge to master gate. What the declarations forbid is read over writes as well as reads: dropping an element a declaration in force still names is destructive, and so is adding a constraint — a not-null constraint, a domain check, a uniqueness rule — that a write declared inside the range would violate, because the release that writes that way is one a rollback can restore, and an additive diff that condemns its inserts turns a recovery into a write outage. The middle item of a migration diffs empty by construction — both forms present, only the writes moving — so the diff alone would pass it unconditionally; what decides it is [the candidate environment](05-environments.md#what-the-candidate-environment-decides), where the run's writes land in the environment's own store and every store declaration in force, read and write, is decided against the state it holds — the past release's predicate over a column the candidate stopped writing is exercised there or it is undecided, and undecided is read at the gate the way a failure is. The drawback falls on small work — renaming a column is three items and three trips through the pipeline. What it makes possible is the rollback in [_Releases_](06-releases.md#rollback), which is otherwise a promise about code made over data that has already changed.

## Work that spans services

Work that spans services needs no new record type. One request can produce four items in four services, and the intent is what joins them — every item already links back to the intent that caused it, so "everything that came from this request" is a query that works today. The four items of a contract migration are the same a few hops longer: two items decomposed from the intent, the brownout raised on the mark's evidence, and the removal on the brownout's window — each link naming the record before it, back to the contract version the intent's own item minted — so the query returns all four, walking the evidence links where it walked the intent link.
