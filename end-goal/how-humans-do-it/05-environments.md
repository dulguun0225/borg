# Environments

Where software runs, and how a change reaches each place it runs.

## Records, and one long-lived branch

Environments are records, not names in code. One record type with a **kind** fixed at creation and three values — production, a deploy target a customer defines, and a [candidate](06-releases.md#what-a-build-is-called-and-when)'s own. [Gate](03-gates.md#where-a-gate-is-and-what-decides-it) thresholds, credential references, and the targets it is reached at are fields of both persistent kinds; the strategy default is production's alone, because a [strategy](03-gates.md#the-rollout-strategy) decides whether a [control](08-operations.md#the-health-monitor) runs and a control exists only where organic traffic does. A candidate's environment holds nothing an owner authored, being created at the gate that decides its deploy and so unable to hold the threshold deciding it. Production exists everywhere, every candidate gets one of its own, and customers define more per [project](11-screens.md#work-ops-factory-people).

An environment names its **targets**, plural — the addresses a deploy is performed against — and they are a field rather than records of their own, nothing holding a reference to one that has to survive an address change: a [deploy record](06-releases.md#the-deploy-record) is keyed by service and environment rather than by target, a mismatch names the address that differs, and [_The independent checker_](08-operations.md#the-independent-checker) reads each in turn. The cost is that an owner declares every target before the factory can deploy into that environment, and a target added at the provider and never declared is deployed to by nothing and read by the independent checker as nothing — a real target running old software named by no mismatch.

The kind is also the seam, two writers never writing a record of the other's kind. An owner writes the persistent kinds on [_Factory_](11-screens.md#work-ops-factory-people): production's at the creation of the project, which an owner does not choose because production exists everywhere, and each further one when they define it. The [deployer](08-operations.md#the-health-monitor) writes a candidate's, creating it at the approval of [Deploy to candidate environment](03-gates.md#deploy-to-candidate-environment) and tearing it down when the [item](01-one-pipeline.md) merges, is dropped, or is superseded by a [re-decomposition](02-intent-into-items.md#decomposition) — the only component that reaches a deploy target at all is the one that reaches environments, and a gate decides an event and edits nothing. What that costs is three creation paths for one record type, so every reader excludes the kinds it does not want, and a torn-down candidate record is kept rather than deleted because the deploy records naming it would otherwise point at nothing.

A persistent environment contains a project — every service in it — while a [release](06-releases.md#the-release-record), a [release number](06-releases.md#the-release-number), a master branch, and a [watch window](08-operations.md#the-watch-window) are all per service. A deploy puts one service's build on one environment, so an environment fed by a twelve-service project has twelve independent promotion paths through it. A candidate's environment contains no project: it is composed for one candidate, which [_An environment per candidate_](#an-environment-per-candidate) sets out. The gate row a customer's environment adds describes that environment; what fires is one decision per deploy into it. A customer's extra environments are deploy targets and not ordered stops — nothing makes a release pass through one before production, and the [production deploy gate](03-gates.md#deploy-to-production) gains no [hold](03-gates.md#what-a-gate-may-change) on another environment's deploy. An owner who wants a human to look at a release running somewhere first adds a [safeguard](09-gate-policy.md#one-shape-across-all-of-them) putting one at that gate (9). What that costs is that a staging environment enforces nothing: a release can reach production having never been deployed there.

Its history is not a field. The deploys that name a record are inbound edges, the arrangement the release record already uses, and [incidents](08-operations.md#incidents) and [rollbacks](06-releases.md#rollback) exist on production environments alone, because the [health monitor](08-operations.md#the-health-monitor) that opens an incident and the strategy that keeps a control both attach to a production deploy and to no other. The cost is that a pre-production environment where something misbehaves has no record of it — what is written there is a rejected candidate or nothing at all.

One long-lived branch is each service's promotion path: master. A candidate has a branch of its own until it merges, is dropped, or is superseded by a re-decomposition. Merging and deploying are separate events and so are separate gates — a merge admits a change to master, a deploy puts a build on an environment, and either can happen without the other. A deploy can be rerun; a merge cannot be unmerged the same way.

A candidate is not a record. It names an item and the build currently in force for it, and a build on a candidate branch belongs to one item, so the pair and the build pick out the same thing — a record per pair would repeat what the build and the item say already. The [criteria](03-gates.md#spec) results and the candidate deploy attach to the build. The branch and the environment are the item's and persist across a rebuild, the environment being a record already. What that costs is that [_The merge queue_](#the-merge-queue)'s rejection has no record of its own: no gate fires, so it is written into [the log](../deferred.md) by the log with the queue as caller and [actor](../deferred.md), the way [a wait no gate could compute](03-gates.md#what-a-gate-may-change) is, and not as a [decision](03-gates.md#where-a-gate-is-and-what-decides-it) — there is no firing to open a row at, the [Merge to master](03-gates.md#merge-to-master) gate's own having closed as an approval, and no [factor vector](04-risk-score.md#factors-at-least) computed for it. It is counted as an attempt against [_the limit_](03-gates.md#the-attempt-limit) at the [stage](02-intent-into-items.md#what-an-item-names) the item is sent back to. What that costs is that [_Factory_](11-screens.md#work-ops-factory-people)'s gate rejection rate, being rejects over firings per gate row, does not see it: one of the three ways an item goes back is absent from the number an owner reads.

A **build** is a record, one per commit built, written by the deployer when it is called to build. Three callers: the implementation stage when it finishes, so the [Implementation gate](03-gates.md#implementation) decides over a build that exists and the [score](04-risk-score.md) has a diff to compute the [change factors](04-risk-score.md#factors-at-least) from; the candidate deploy where a rebuild is needed; and the merge queue at re-verification, which builds the candidate branch onto the master it will actually merge into. A rebuild is a new build, so an item has as many builds as it was built and the release names the last one — which is what makes the commit that was verified being the commit that merges a property of the queue. Every record naming a build says which: a gate decision names the one in force at the firing, a factor vector is computed from that same build, a [consumer contract](07-contracts.md#what-a-consumer-declares) names the one it was derived from. The commit is a field of the build and not a record, one commit per build and nothing pointing at a commit that has no build. What the build costs is that a component named for reaching an environment is called once per item where no environment is reached, and that an item rebuilt four times leaves three builds nothing points at.

What a run on a candidate environment produced attaches to the build — each criterion decided, and each consumer contract checked against the exchanges observed there — written by the deployer when the run finishes, and not a record of its own: the identity is the build plus the criterion id, and which environment the run happened on is the item's, which the build names. The build could not name one itself — it is written when the implementation stage finishes, one gate above the approval that creates the environment. The producer's own [contract diff](07-contracts.md#enforcement) is not among them. It needs no run and no environment, and a verdict stored at run time is not a check made when the gate fires, which is what [_Two baselines, kept apart_](07-contracts.md#what-a-consumer-declares) requires. What is written is what was observed; what is decided is decided at the gate.

## An environment per candidate

Every candidate gets an environment of its own, created from master plus that candidate and torn down when the item merges, is dropped, or is superseded by a [re-decomposition](02-intent-into-items.md#decomposition). Nothing is shared, so nothing queues behind anything: a candidate being repaired delays nobody, and a candidate waiting on a human delays only itself. Nothing shares because nothing else is candidate-fed — the environments a customer defines are deploy targets for master, so no persistent slot exists for candidates to take turns on.

The environment is composed for the candidate running in it — the [_current releases_](06-releases.md#the-deploy-record) of its dependencies, what is running rather than what is newest, never another service's candidate, and wherever those dependencies are, a service in another project included. That is what makes a twelve-service project testing twelve items at once safe, and it is only safe because no item may break a contract. What it was composed from is a field the deployer writes at creation and rewrites when the environment is recomposed at a re-verification, naming each dependency and the release of it that was current then; no deploy record is written for a dependency put in place that way, a deploy record being what a gate decided and there being one gate here, over the item's own build. The cost is that a field the deployer writes about its own work can disagree with what is actually on that environment, and nothing checks it — the independent checker reads production targets only.

The cost is infrastructure per item in progress rather than one shared environment per service. It is the factory's cost and not a human's, and it is the whole of what removes the queue: where the score or a safeguard puts a human on a candidate, that human's delay is that item's alone, where a single shared environment would have imposed it on every item behind them.

## The merge queue

Candidates verified in parallel cannot all fast-forward, because each was built on a master that has since moved. So merging is a queue: a candidate entering it re-verifies against master plus every candidate ahead of it, and fast-forwards only if that passes. A failure invalidates the speculation behind it, and those candidates re-verify against the master that actually resulted.

A candidate that fails its own re-verification — against the master that actually resulted, not against a speculation ahead of it — failed on its merits, and the queue rejects it: the item [goes back up](03-gates.md#going-back-up) [the pipeline](01-one-pipeline.md) to Implementation, the queue naming no other stage and there being none between, counts an attempt there against [_the limit_](03-gates.md#the-attempt-limit), and the score learns from it the way it learns from a [reject](03-gates.md#what-a-gate-may-change) and not from a hold. That is the stop the [Merge to master](03-gates.md#merge-to-master) gate can no longer give, having already approved, and without it a candidate failing here would have no path at all. Candidates behind a failure count nothing, for the same reason they re-verify without an attempt counted against them: they failed because of someone else.

The queue restores what one shared environment used to provide — the commit that was verified is the commit that merges. It costs compute on speculative runs that a failure ahead of them throws away, which is the better of the two: the shared environment imposed the same serialization on human time, and this imposes it on machine time.

Its order is settable, like the queue at any gate. Reordering changes when a candidate re-verifies, never what it has to pass. The settable order is one priority field of the item, written through [dispatch](02-intent-into-items.md#dispatch) with [Work](11-screens.md#work-ops-factory-people) as the caller, and it orders every queue an item waits in as an item — this one and the queues at the gates up to and including Merge to master. It orders nothing after the merge: numbered releases waiting to deploy are ordered by the release number and by nothing else, [_the revert a rollback is waiting on_](08-operations.md#overlapping-windows) excepted. So an owner who rushes an item to the front here has rushed it at every gate it has left, and has no way at all to reorder a deploy — an urgent fix behind three numbered releases waits for them.

The queue is a component and not a record. Its membership is the items whose [stage](02-intent-into-items.md#what-an-item-names) says Merge to master approved them and whose fast-forward has not happened, its order is that priority and then the time of the approval in [the log](../deferred.md), and its speculative re-verifications are its own state, read by nothing outside it. The two outcomes that outlast a run are records already: a fast-forward writes the release record, and a failure on the candidate's own merits writes a decision. What that costs is that nothing records how long a candidate waited, so why something took a day is reconstructed from the approval time against the release record's own.

Two facts about master are queries and not fields, for the same reason. Master's head is the commit of that service's highest-numbered release, and empty until the first one. The next number is one above the highest among that service's release records — numbers are never reused and a rolled-back release keeps its own, so the maximum is always right, and the queue's per-service ordering is what stops two candidates reading one. A counter beside either would be the same fact written twice at one event, and a lost counter write mints a duplicate.

## What the candidate environment decides

[The graph](../what-the-factory-does.md#tight-integration) is not uniform. Up to the merge, what moves is a candidate and deploys are plain — no strategy, no rollout, and no [_watch window_](08-operations.md#the-watch-window), because a candidate environment has no organic traffic and a comparison computed from one human exercising a screen is noise that looks like evidence. What the environment decides is the criteria: the candidate runs on production-like infrastructure against the current releases of its dependencies, and every consumer contract is checked against it there. Deciding them is running the encoding [_Implementation_](03-gates.md#implementation) authored beside the code, and a criterion that fails is a rejection at the Merge to master gate on the same terms a consumer contract failing there is.

A criterion has a third outcome and it is not a kind of pass. An encoding that produced a failure and a pass over the same build decided nothing — what a repeated run measures is the repetition — so the criterion is **undecided** for that build, and undecided is read at the Merge to master gate the way a failure is. Recording it as a pass is what would make the verdict rest on nothing, since a passing criterion is the whole of what that gate reads about the item's own behaviour. What it costs is that an encoding whose own defect has nothing to do with the change stops the item, and the way out is to author the encoding again rather than to run it again: retrying until green is available to nobody, the factory included, because the factory is what would otherwise do it at scale.

Merge to master is where a candidate becomes a release and gets its number. Everything from there is machine: numbering, strategy selection, rollout, monitoring, rollback.

The verdict is score-gated like every other gate, so it is not the last place a human decides and there is no last one: the same score decides at each gate whether a human decides there, and a safeguard (9) puts one back. Where one is there, what they are doing is [UAT](../what-humans-do.md) (7) — the environment is no longer named for it, because in the steady state nobody is there. Where it auto-passes, the verdict is the factory's own, taken against acceptance criteria a human already confirmed (6).

The alternative was to separate that verdict by origin — permanent for human-originated features, auto-passable for factory-originated fixes — and that is the second, invisible path the pipeline forbids, separated by a worse predictor than the score's own factors. Scoring it costs this: a change can reach production with nobody having watched it run. The watch window covers half of that and says so: it catches a change that misbehaves, never one that behaves perfectly and is the wrong thing. What catches a wrong thing built well is the criteria confirmed at the [Spec gate](03-gates.md#spec), end users (4, 5), and [a human undoing the change after it shipped](../what-humans-do.md) (10) — and an owner who wants more adds it back per service or per area with a safeguard.
