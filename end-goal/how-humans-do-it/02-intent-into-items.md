# Intent into items

How what arrives becomes the items the factory builds, and everything that happens to it on the way.

## Intake

What arrives is an **intent**: a request from an owner (1), the end-user [_reports_](#reports) a grouper turned into one (4, 5), or something the factory found itself, raised by a **detector** — a comparison still running after its window closed, a consumer breaking in its own error rate. One record, three sources, and one writer — intake writes every intent, and the three sources are three callers of one entrance rather than three components writing the same record. What it writes at that event is the source, the actor (seam 1 of [_Deferred, but not designed out_](../deferred.md)), whatever per-intent constraint arrived with the request, and for an intent the factory raised, a link to the evidence that raised it. An intent arrives unrefined, and nothing is judged on the way in, because judging it is what the interview is for.

It also writes the **originating project** where a source supplies one — the project a request was raised under, the project of the service a grouped report came from, the project named in a detector's evidence. That field has one job: it is where [_The cut_](#the-cut) places a service the work creates, there being no existing service to reach a project through. It is not a claim about where the work is, because a contract migration is one intent whose three items may be in two projects, and it is not what scopes the constraints [_The interview_](#the-interview) reads. Where no source supplies one the field stays unwritten, and intake fills it once if the cut has to place a created service and nothing else answers; it is never rewritten, so an approval keeps pointing at what was approved. Putting the project on the intent and not on the item is not a contradiction of [_What an item names_](#what-an-item-names): an item names a service and reaches a project through it, and an intent names no service.

One writer costs a dependency edge from the far end of the pipeline back to its start: operations calls intake to raise what it found. What several writers would cost is one record's rules — the actor always populated, the chain, the links — implemented once per writer and drifting apart, on the record every other record links back to.

The intent is what the graph joins on. Every item links back to it, which is why one request producing four items in four services needs no record type of its own — the same point [_Work that spans services_](07-contracts.md#work-that-spans-services) makes from the other end.

The factory's own source repeats where the other two do not: a comparison still running is still running tomorrow, and a consumer breaking in its own error rate goes on breaking. So a detector raises an intent for a condition and not for an observation — before raising one it looks for an intent on the same evidence that has not finished, and attaches to that one instead. The evidence is what keys it: this service, this consumer, this contract, this release. Keyed too finely it raises an intent per observation; too coarsely it attaches two problems to one intent, which the cut then has to split.

### Constraints and the design system

Constraints (2) arrive here too and are not intents: they are never cut into items and never ship. One record with a reach field rather than two kinds — a constraint names what it binds, and the four values it may take are the ones duty 2 sets out in [_What humans do_](../what-humans-do.md), which groups the three widest as **standing** and leaves **per-intent** the narrowest, arriving with a single request and binding every item cut from it. Intake writes it at arrival whichever surface an owner supplied it on, because the constraint's own rules — the actor, the chain — are implemented once rather than once per entrance, and a second write withdraws it. It is never edited: correcting one is a withdrawal and a second record, so what was in force when a stage was authored is reconstructible from arrival and withdrawal alone.

What the reach decides is what the factory reads when it drafts, and drafting is per item: the constraints in force over the factory, over the item's service's project, over the item's area or any area above it, plus whatever arrived with this intent. The interview reads less than that, and deliberately — the factory's own and the intent's own, and not the project's or the area's, because before the cut which project the work is in is a guess and an intent's three items may not share one.

A constraint's other field is its **form**, and it decides which gate can reject on one. A build the factory can diff is decided against the implementation and rejects at [_Implementation_](03-gates.md#implementation). A predicate the factory can decide against an observed run is decided on [_the candidate environment_](05-environments.md#what-the-candidate-environment-decides) beside the acceptance criteria, and rejects at the merge gate on the same terms. A document is decided by nobody, and what enforces it is a pin (9) putting a human at a gate. The cost is that an owner who supplies a predicate learns of a breach one gate later than one who supplies a build, and everything spent between the two is thrown away.

A design system is not a record of its own: it is a constraint whose reach is one project and whose subject is what every screen is built from, and its form field decides everything below. It is the standing constraint a project with a user interface always has, and it is chosen as much as supplied: the project uses one the factory ships with or one an owner uploads. One the factory ships with is content of the product rather than a record — picking it writes a constraint naming it, the way a fleet entry names a credential the factory does not hold, and it moves only when the owner upgrades the factory. What is uploaded decides what can ever be checked against it. As code — tokens and components with a build — it is diffable, and a screen that departs from it fails mechanically. As a document — an export, a page of rules — there is no build, nothing checks the result, and what enforces it is a pin (9) putting a human at the gate. Nothing about that is particular to design: no standing constraint is checked mechanically unless it arrives in a form a machine can read, which is why the compliance officer holding (2) over a regulated area holds a pin with it.

So a designer's say continues after the gate they decided at, but unevenly. The screen's state flow confirmed at [_Spec_](03-gates.md#spec) (6) is authored as a state machine and enforced at [_Implementation_](03-gates.md#implementation); what the screen looks like is checked by nobody unless the design system came as code. The cheap form requires a human at a pinned gate for as long as the pin lasts; the enforceable one requires a designer to produce code.

### Reports

An end user's bug report (4) or complaint (5) is a **report**, and a report is not an intent. The way in is thin and embedded in the deployed software: what it writes is a report, into the **report store**, one of its own sized for what a population of end users sends rather than for what a graph joins on. A grouper reads that store and calls intake once per group, so five hundred reports of one slow button are one intent and not five hundred.

This source alone has a record in front of the intent. An owner's request is one thing because one person typed it, and a factory finding is keyed by its evidence. Only an end user's report arrives in a quantity nobody upstream has collapsed, in words the factory has to read to know whether two of them are one problem.

A report raises an intent on arrival rather than waiting for a batch: the first of a group raises it, and later matching ones attach. Nothing waits for a count, because grouping is deduplication and not triage — one report from one end user is an intent, and the count is evidence on it rather than a rank: nothing orders work by it on its own, though a human reordering a queue can read it. A batch would group better and delay every fix by the length of the interval.

A report is marked with the intent it was grouped into and kept. Deleting one on grouping would cost the measurement this store makes possible — the rate of reports before and after the release meant to fix what they describe — so what removes a report is a retention setting an owner authors, and never the grouping. That setting is a field of the one policy list the factory owns, which [_Gate policy_](09-gate-policy.md#what-is-not-in-it) sets out, and its only reader is the report store — the component that writes a report is the only one that may remove one. Where an owner authors nothing, reports are kept for the life of the install: no outcome teaches a retention period, so there is nothing for the score to supply.

Grouping takes no gate. The interview, the cut, and [_Spec_](03-gates.md#spec) all read what it produced, which is the argument [_Dispatch_](#dispatch) makes for itself. The two ways it goes wrong are not symmetric, so the grouper prefers to group: two problems in one intent are separated one stage down, where the cut yields an item each or a spec comes back too large, while duplicates that were never grouped are separated by nobody — each takes its own interview, its own thread, and its own item, several of them on the same code and serialized behind each other in [_The merge queue_](05-environments.md#the-merge-queue).

A later report attaches to the intent and never rewrites it. What refines an intent is attached to it and every reader reads both, so the five hundredth report changes what is built by being read rather than by editing the record — which is what keeps an approval pointing at what was approved. The cut is the boundary. Before it, the grouper may still split a group it got wrong; after it, a report matching work already cut attaches and raises the count, and one that does not is a new intent, linked to the first as a recurrence. A report arriving after the fix shipped is that same rule: the thread is finished, so evidence that the fix did not work is a new intent and never a reopening. What the boundary costs is a run of reports just after a narrow cut showing as two linked threads where a human would call it one problem.

For an intent grouped from reports, the reports are what the interview and the spec are authored against and the intent's own statement summarizes them; for an owner's request the statement is the thing itself.

A report that is never grouped is work nobody sees, which is how a store in front of a pipeline fails wherever it is built. So every report is either linked to the intent it was grouped into or counted as ungrouped, and that count is a number on [_Factory_](11-surfaces.md#work-ops-factory-people). One report appears in [_Work_](11-surfaces.md#work-ops-factory-people) under its intent and nowhere else: a browsable list of reports would put end-user volume on the surface that is one item per thread, and make the grouping a human's job again.

## The interview

The factory runs the interview and the factory ends it. It asks (3) until it has enough to cut the intent and author a spec per item whose criteria fit the six patterns [_Spec_](03-gates.md#spec) sets out — and what ends the questioning is having enough to author, not a human declaring the intent clear. An owner cannot know what those patterns will demand of an answer; the factory is the one that has them.

The interview is not a stage of its own. It is the state an intent is in, which is why it has no gate and no artifact a gate decides over: intake writes the intent, the interview refines it, the cut turns it into items. So the state is a field on the intent, and it has three values. An intent arrives **unrefined**. It is **refined** when the factory has enough to cut it and author a spec per item, which is the same thing as ready to cut and needs no second name. It is **escalated** where the rounds exceeded [_the attempt bound_](03-gates.md#the-attempt-bound), and that value is written rather than recomputed from the round count: the bound is authored with gate policy (8) and changes, and a decision read back against a value that was not in force when it was taken is not the decision that was taken.

The state is the intent's own and stops at the cut. What comes after is read from the items — [_A partial intent_](#a-partial-intent) is computed from whether they all reached a release, and whether an intent was cut at all is answered by the items that exist. So the record keeps one writer: a transition is a write to the intent and goes through intake like every other, and the cut writes items and never writes back.

What is written once is the statement and whatever is attached to it, which is what keeps an approval pointing at what was approved; the state advances. Nothing reads the state back to justify a decision — it says where the intent is, which is what [_Work_](11-surfaces.md#work-ops-factory-people) answers and what puts an agent on the intents still unrefined.

A **question** is a record, one per question asked, attached to the intent and naming the round it was asked in, with the answer written onto it when the answer arrives. Intake writes both, called by the factory at the question and by Work at the answer, because a question is attached to the intent and takes the intent's one writer — the arrangement [_Intake_](#intake) already makes for every write to that record, the interview being a state and not a component able to write one. It is not an artifact: nothing gates it, no attempt is counted on it, and the spec is still what the refinement produces. What that costs is a record written twice, so a reader tells an unanswered question from an answered one by a field rather than by the record existing — and the answer is write-once, so an owner who answers badly waits to be asked again.

How many rounds have been asked is a field of the intent, written by intake at each round like every other write to it, and it is what [_the attempt bound_](03-gates.md#the-attempt-bound) is compared against here. Counting the questions instead would count a round that asked three as three, and the bound counts rounds.

Questions appear in [_Work_](11-surfaces.md#work-ops-factory-people) under the intent that raised them, and are answered there. The spec is the artifact, so there is no interview gate and none is missing: approving the spec ratifies the refinement that produced it, which is what [_Where a gate is, and what decides it_](03-gates.md#where-a-gate-is-and-what-decides-it) already assumes.

The drawback is that an owner cannot shorten it. An intent whose questions go unanswered does not proceed, and the end condition belonging to the factory is what makes that wait the owner's to end.

Two things go wrong with an interview, and only one of them spends anything. An owner who stops answering spends nothing — no build, no environment, no agent is running — so no bound applies to that wait and none should: a bound would turn their silence into an escalation (12) they have to clear anyway, which is the human work the factory exists to remove. An interview that is answered and still never reaches enough to author does spend, round after round, and it has [_the attempt bound_](03-gates.md#the-attempt-bound) like any other stretch of work the factory can fail at — the same parameter, authored with gate policy (8), counting rounds here rather than retries. Exceeding it appears in Work as an escalation (12), the factory saying it cannot refine this one; it still takes no gate, the spec being the artifact that ratifies the refinement. A human who answers clears the escalation and the count starts again, because the escalation is what put them there and answering it is a decision to spend more — the bound is still the only limit the factory holds, and an owner who keeps clearing is choosing to spend past it, limited then by the quota on the provider account behind the agent. The drawback is that a bound low enough to stop an interview going in circles will also stop one that was about to converge.

## The cut

One intent becomes one item or several — one per service the work changes, three where a contract migration is what the work is. The cut is where [_No single item may break a contract_](07-contracts.md#no-single-item-may-break-a-contract) is applied rather than discovered: "an item that cannot ship by itself was cut wrong" is a statement about this stage, and until the cut got a gate of its own, nothing ratified what it produced.

A service the work changes may not exist yet, and nothing about the cut changes: one item creates it, and whatever calls it is a second item declared to wait on the first. The cut writes the service record in the same write as that item, because an item names one service and the record has to exist for the item's only outbound link to point at anything. Its other writer is an owner at [_Factory_](11-surfaces.md#work-ops-factory-people), putting K and the watch window's parameters on it (8), and the seam between the two is the field: the cut writes the service's identity and never a parameter, an owner writes parameters and never creates a service. A service is one repository with one long-lived branch inside it, and no repository holds two: master, the number, and the queue's ordering are all per service, and two services sharing a repository would give it two divergent trunks, so a build of either would be made from a tree that contains the other's stale code. The drawback is that a twelve-service project is twelve repositories, and a customer arriving with a monorepo has a split to perform that nothing in the factory does for them. Master itself does not exist until the first release: the implementation role commits the candidate branch with no base, and the queue's first fast-forward is what creates master. Nothing writes the service's place in an environment either — a deploy record is keyed by service and environment, so a service is in an environment by having been deployed there. The item ships by itself, a service nothing calls yet being exactly the kind of thing that can. The drawback is that the factory's weakest measurement applies to the release the rest of the work waits on: a first release is [_straight_](03-gates.md#the-rollout-strategy) whatever the score would prefer, nothing can close its window [_clean_](08-operations.md#the-watch-window), where no threshold is pinned (9) it is [_unmeasured altogether_](08-operations.md#the-health-signal), and it has no [_rollback target_](06-releases.md#rollback). Each of those is stated where it arises, and this is where they arrive together.

Shipping by itself is a minimum and not a size — it admits an item that touches forty files and one that changes a string. What sizes an item between them is a target the score supplies, which an owner authors or pins per area (9) with the rest of gate policy (8). It learns from what a bad size does in each direction, and both are already among the numbers [_Factory_](11-surfaces.md#work-ops-factory-people) reports: cut too coarse shows as attempt-bound escalations (12), with everything spent on the item thrown away, and cut too fine shows as cost per feature and rework rate — an environment, a spec, four gates, and a release number for a one-line change. The minimums do not move with the target. It ships by itself, and no single item may break a contract, which is three items however small the target.

The cut also records the order. Where one item cannot be verified until another has shipped — the producing release of a migration — that dependency is declared here, and both deploy gates hold on it: [_Deploy to candidate environment_](03-gates.md#deploy-to-candidate-environment) until the dependency is live, [_Deploy to production_](03-gates.md#deploy-to-production) if it has stopped being. Ordering is a property of the cut, not something discovered at deploy time.

Decomposition is the cut's own gate, scored like every other, and it fires where the cut yielded more than one item. What is approved is the set: how many items, which service each changes, and what waits on what. A rejection re-cuts the set rather than sending one item back, because what the gate decides on is the cut and not an item. Edit in place is a human re-cutting by hand.

One verdict covers the whole cut, however many services it changes. Splitting it up — one verdict per service, each holder deciding only the items in theirs — would rebuild here the wait [_Who owns a contract_](07-contracts.md#who-owns-a-contract) refused: approvals that must all arrive before anything proceeds, in a graph where a four-service cut is ordinary, and the attempt bound turning a long enough wait into escalations (12). A holder who wants to decide at the cut for their own service gets that with a pin (9) on their area, which is what a pin does everywhere else. The drawback is that they first see the work one stage down, with the authoring already done — never blind, since the item links back to its intent and Work shows the sibling threads under the same decision, but reading a spec rather than a proposal.

The cut writes the items, and the gate decides over records that already exist. Every other authoring gate does — Spec, Implementation plan, Tasks, and Implementation each decide over an artifact already written — and the alternative gives the cut decision fields describing the set it proposes, which is what an item already records. It would also split one record's writer in two: a cut yielding one item fires no gate, so items would appear at approval where a gate fired and at the cut where none did.

A rejected set is superseded rather than discarded. Each rejected item points at the items of the re-cut that replaced it — a list, since a re-cut may replace four items with two — written by the cut in the same write that creates the replacements, so what was cut wrong is readable beside what replaced it rather than only in the decision's feedback. Where a re-cut replaced an item with nothing the pointer stays unwritten, and what says why is the item's superseded stage beside the decision that rejected the set. What the pair of choices costs is item records that no gate approved, a superseded set per rejected round, and a service record the cut wrote for an item the re-cut did not replace, which no live item then names: every reader of items — [_Work_](11-surfaces.md#work-ops-factory-people) first — has to tell a live item from one of those.

The Spec gate cannot do this job — it is per item, so approving one item's spec never ratifies the decomposition that produced four. Without a gate here, a wrong cut is discovered several specs later and re-cutting means abandoning items that were already approved. So the gate fires where there is a set to ratify and nowhere else: a cut that yields one item produced none, and putting a gate in front of every intent — most of them single-item — would be a decision about nothing. Scoring it was the cheaper defence and it is the wrong one: a gate the score auto-passes is still a gate every builder implements and every intent passes through. What the condition costs is the cut that should have yielded four and yielded one, which now reaches a human one stage down as a spec too large, at [_Spec_](03-gates.md#spec) rather than here.

### What an item names

An item names one service, and its area is a field of the item that the cut writes. An area is free to cut across services, so it cannot be reached through the service an item names — the work is in a part of the software an owner grouped, and the cut is what knows which part. The project is not a field: a service is in exactly one, so an item naming its own would let the two disagree, and a service moved between projects would leave every item naming the old one. What that costs is a join — a pin (9) or a [_scope_](01-one-pipeline.md) drawn on an area reads the item alone, and one drawn on a project reads the item's service.

Areas form a chain, each naming the area or the project it lies inside (9), so which of two is narrower is a link rather than a judgment over prose. The cut writes the narrowest area in the chain whose declaration covers the work, and only an area inside the project of the service the item names, so the item's area and its service agree by construction. A pin or a scope drawn on any area in that chain reaches the item: the gate and dispatch follow it from the item's area up to the project. Without the chain an owner who declared a narrower area inside a pinned one would lose the pin the moment the cut wrote the narrower name, and nothing in the tree may remove a human from a gate. Two groupings that genuinely cross-cut need a third area declared for the intersection.

One intent's items may be in more than one project. A contract migration is one intent and three items, and [_Contracts_](07-contracts.md#two-versioned-things) puts a contract's consumers anywhere in the factory, so the cut writes items wherever the work is and the intent stays what joins them. What that costs is that the three items are decided under two projects' gate thresholds and two sets of pins, and that a fleet whose entries are scoped to one project cannot be dispatched onto the item in the other — which shows in Work as an item that does not advance, indistinguishable from a fleet too small.

Which stage an item is at is a field on the item, and **dispatch** is what writes it — every stage reports a transition to it, and the component that reads the field on every move is the one that keeps it. Two more fields go with it, written at the same transitions and both kept per stage: how many attempts each stage has taken, and what each stage spent. The current stage's count is what a gate compares against [_the attempt bound_](03-gates.md#the-attempt-bound), because an attempt that failed before a gate fired wrote no decision to count, and it is where an agent's failed attempt, a gate's reject, and [_the merge queue_](05-environments.md#the-merge-queue)'s rejection all report; a hold reports nothing, and neither does a candidate re-verifying behind someone else's failure. A human taking an escalated item over (12) clears the escalation and the bound counts from there, the way it does when an owner answers an escalated [_interview_](#the-interview), and the attempts already taken stay on the stage. Kept rather than reset at each advance, those counts are what [_Factory_](11-surfaces.md#work-ops-factory-people)'s rework rate reads. Dispatch matches the stage against the role and reads the item alone. Deriving it instead — a query over which artifacts exist and which gate decisions passed — would put that join in every reader and would not remove the field: an item that exceeded [_the attempt bound_](03-gates.md#the-attempt-bound) is escalated (12), and that value is written for the reason the interview's is, that a bound authored with gate policy (8) changes and a decision read back against a value that was not in force when it was taken is not the decision that was taken. A superseded item is a value here too, and it is what tells one from an item the cut wrote a moment ago, neither having an artifact yet. An item can also stop for good without reaching a release, and **dropped** is the value that says so — written by dispatch with Work as the caller, where a human stops one that escalated (12) and nobody took over. Merged or dropped is what frees an item's branch and its environment, and [_An environment per candidate_](05-environments.md#an-environment-per-candidate) tears one down on exactly those two events; an escalated item holds both until a human ends it either way.

The item has two writers and the seam is the event: the cut writes it once, at the cut — the intent, the service, the area, the branch name, and what it waits on — and never writes an item again; every later write is dispatch's. An owner reordering a queue writes the priority through dispatch rather than beside it, the way Work calls the interview to answer a question. One writer after the cut costs a dependency edge from every stage back to dispatch, which is the cost [_Intake_](#intake) already accepts on the intent and for the same reason — the record's rules implemented once rather than once per stage — and it puts a match the tree calls not a judgment on the write path of every item, unavailable exactly when nothing can move. What the fields cost is that they can disagree with the artifacts and with the decisions: a transition that fails to write leaves an item naming a stage its artifacts do not show, and a reader asking why an item escalated still reads the decisions to see what the attempts were.

## A partial intent

An intent whose items did not all ship is a **partial intent**, and it is an outcome rather than a fault: one item exceeds the attempt bound and appears as an escalation (12), or a human vetoes it, while its siblings are already live. Nothing writes it — an intent is partial when at least one of its items stopped without reaching production and at least one sibling is live, an item being live when its release is the one its service's production deploy record names. An intent whose items are all still moving is in progress rather than partial, one where nothing shipped is stopped, and one whose superseded items were replaced by a re-cut is judged on the replacements. It is not terminal either: a human taking over an escalated item (12) can finish it, and the intent stops being partial with no event anywhere, which is why a field would have to be unwritten and is not a field. The cost is three joins — intent to items, item to release, release to that service's production deploy record — so partial is only as cheap as those inbound edges are indexed, and an item merely held at the production deploy gate never shows as partial however long it waits. The shipped ones stay shipped. Each was cut to ship by itself and is worth something by itself — and where it is not, the cut was wrong, which is what the gate above exists to catch. Undoing the whole thing is one revert item per shipped sibling, joined by the same intent, and never a single undo.

The declared order is what keeps that survivable. An item that fails stops only its dependents: whatever it depended on shipped before it, and whatever depended on it was still held at a deploy gate. So a partial intent is a feature half delivered, not production half broken.

## Dispatch

With the cut approved, the factory puts its own agents onto the items — a model in a role with a scope, as [_One pipeline_](01-one-pipeline.md) has it. Dispatch gets no gate: it decides who authors, and everything authored is reviewed at a gate anyway, where the score reads the authorship prior of the model that wrote it.

What limits how many items move at once is infrastructure — an environment per candidate — and that cost is the factory's rather than a human's. Where a human authors a stage instead of an agent (11, 12), nothing about dispatch changes: authorship is an attribute of the stage, and the item is still one thread.

Factory is where an owner watches this: [_the fleet_](10-fleet.md), what each agent is doing, and how well.
