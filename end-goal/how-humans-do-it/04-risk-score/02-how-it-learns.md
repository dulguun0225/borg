# How it learns

The score is learned, not fixed. Every bad decision feeds back and refines it: an [auto-passed](../03-gates/01-where-a-gate-is-and-what-decides-it.md) change that a human undoes (10), a low-risk change its analysis window rolled back, a gate the factory would have passed but a human rejected. Outcome feedback is the sharpest signal but not the only one — any source that improves the score is admissible, and the input set stays open.

A supplied value moves on both ends of its own row, and [gate policy](../09-gate-policy.md#what-is-in-it)'s table is what says where the second end is: every row there states what goes wrong at each end, and both are evidence. One end is something going wrong — a rollback, a human's undo, a [crossing](../08-operations.md#the-analysis-window) of the boundary the [health monitor](../08-operations.md#the-health-monitor) could have seen and did not, an [item](../01-one-pipeline.md) the [attempt limit](../03-gates/05-the-attempt-limit.md) gave up on. The other is the parameter costing more than it returns, which is observable for most of them: attempts nothing has ever needed, a [cap](../08-operations.md#the-analysis-window) far longer than any window of that service takes to resolve, a [window's size](../08-operations.md#the-analysis-window) finer than its traffic can rule anything out at. Reading only the first end would make every value move one way and stay there, and a value that only tightens ends up costing what it was set to protect against — the clearest case being an [analysis window](../08-operations.md#the-analysis-window) so fine it never resolves, watching every release for the whole cap and catching nothing.

Calibration also reads each factor's distribution over the decisions that named it, against that factor's own history. A factor whose distribution has moved is measuring something other than what the formula was calibrated on — an agent that learned what the factor rewards, an area whose shape changed, a fault in what supplies it — and outcomes alone discover that slowly, one release at a time against hundreds of decisions already taken. A factor found drifted takes the treatment [an unavailable factor](01-factors-at-least.md) takes, resolved toward the gate and named on the vector, until the formula is recalibrated over it. What it costs is what that treatment always costs: a drifted factor puts humans at gates across the factory at once, and the vector naming it is what tells that apart from the score changing its mind.

Two of them have no second end here and are stated as such rather than left to be discovered. No outcome says [the confidence a comparison must reach](../08-operations.md#the-analysis-window) was too high: what would show it is a rollback that turned out unnecessary, and the one thing that establishes a failed release was fine — a human [marking the rollback as not caused by the release](../08-operations.md#overlapping-windows) — says the comparison was confounded, nothing about how confident it should have been, so it excludes the rollback from what learns and teaches the confidence nothing. And the other end of the [item-size target](../02-intent-into-items/03-decomposition/README.md) is cost per feature and rework rate, both of which are [_Factory_](../11-screens.md#work-ops-factory-people)'s to report and neither of which anything measures yet. Both are allowed to move one way because neither compounds — the traffic a comparison needs grows as the log of the confidence, where it grows as the inverse square of the size — so an owner may have to author over one of them, which [authoring being an override](../09-gate-policy.md#one-shape-across-all-of-them) is exactly the case for.

The [risk threshold](../09-gate-policy.md#what-is-in-it) is a third exception and not for want of an observable. Its own row states the loose end plainly — gate more and the human work the factory exists to remove comes back — and how often a row puts a human at it is a count, needing no outcome and no counterfactual. What it is not is evidence about safety, and the difference from the two above is the difference between protection gone worthless and protection gone expensive: a window too fine to resolve rules nothing out, so loosening it gives up nothing, where a human at a gate really does read the change and really does stop some of them. Loosening the threshold on its cost alone would be the score deciding that human review is not worth its price, which is a judgment about what the factory is for rather than a reading of what happened — and the design already answers a cost complaint the other way, by having the owner author the value (8). So the threshold moves down on outcomes and up on one thing only, which is the next paragraph.

Scoring on the author reinforces itself if left alone: a distrusted author is gated more, gated work draws more scrutiny, more scrutiny finds more faults, and distrust is confirmed. The factory holds out a random sample — auto-passing what it would have gated, under the longest analysis window there is — to keep unbiased signal on the authors and areas it has stopped trusting. How much it holds out is the **held-out sample rate**, authored with the rest of [gate policy](../09-gate-policy.md#what-is-in-it) (8) rather than chosen by the score, for the reason every other bound there exists: the rate decides how often a change the score wanted gated reaches production with nobody at the gate, and a mechanism that sets its own rate of doing so is bounded by nothing. Where an owner authors none the score supplies it, the same division the [window's size](../08-operations.md#the-analysis-window) uses, and a [safeguard](../09-gate-policy.md#one-shape-across-all-of-them) (9) is a ceiling over it — sampling less is a human kept at a gate.

A held-out window that timed out is not evidence for raising a threshold. The sample exists to produce an outcome, and the exit that reports no outcome reports none here either: counting it as good would put back exactly what the sample was built to remove — an outcome recorded only for what the factory let through, this time as silence read as success. What that costs is the direction it leaves: a quiet service produces held-out releases that teach nothing, so on a service too quiet to close a window on evidence the threshold can fall and cannot rise — the ratchet [_How it learns_](02-how-it-learns.md) already names in the other parameters, and the reason an owner may have to author over it.

The sample is also the only evidence there is for raising a threshold, which is more than the paragraph above asks of it. A change that was gated and turned out well is consistent with three things at once: it was never risky, the human at the gate caught what would have made it risky, or its author was more careful for knowing a human would read it — and no record tells those apart, so none of them is evidence the gate was unnecessary. What does tell them apart is a change the score wanted gated and did not gate, and the sample is the only thing that produces one. So a factory whose sample never selects has a threshold that can fall and cannot rise: it tightens on every outcome it gets wrong and never loosens on one it gets right, the same shape as a [window](../08-operations.md#the-analysis-window) whose size only ever narrows. That is worth saying because the sample reads like a fairness mechanism for authors the factory has stopped trusting, and it is also the only reason half the calibration is possible at all.

The sample is the score's own to hold out of, and it reaches nothing an owner safeguarded. A safeguard (9) can only add, and a gate held always-on is a human added; a sample that could pass one would be the single mechanism in the design that removes a human from a gate — exactly what a safeguard exists to prevent. So what the sample may auto-pass is what the score itself would have gated on a vector it could compute — never the human [an unavailable factor](01-factors-at-least.md) resolved to, the score being in no position to auto-pass what it could not read. The drawback: the areas an owner distrusts most are the ones it learns least about.

The selection is recorded on the decisions rather than as a set of its own, in two fields that must not be one. **Held out** says the score selected this [item](../01-one-pipeline.md), written on every decision on it from the selection onward, so an item selected once auto-passes every gate the score itself would have gated — never one [an unavailable factor](01-factors-at-least.md) resolved to a human — and reaches production with a human removed at each of them. **Why it auto-passed** says, at this firing, whether the auto-pass came from the number being under the threshold or from the selection, and reads threshold at a gate the score would have passed anyway. The health monitor runs the window to the [cap](../08-operations.md#the-analysis-window) on the selection and not on the provenance, because what the sample is evidence about is a [release](../06-releases.md#the-release-record) reaching production with a human removed somewhere above it. A held-out release also takes a strategy that keeps a [control](../08-operations.md#the-health-monitor) wherever there is a build to start one from, whatever the vector's impact half would have picked: the sample exists to produce outcome evidence, and a deploy [without a control](../03-gates/02-the-rollout-strategy.md) leaves only [the weak fallback](../08-operations.md#the-health-monitor). What that costs: a second set of instances for a window that runs to the longest [exit](../08-operations.md#the-analysis-window) there is, and a sample measured better than the population it represents — so its evidence supports that a comparison was available, not that an unsampled release on the same author would have shown the same. A service's first release has no control whatever the score prefers, so the sample cannot usefully select one.
