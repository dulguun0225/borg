# Open

Twenty-seven questions, all raised by the review pass on 2026-08-16 and none by the interview, which finished with nothing open. A question belongs here when an owner is who decides it and the tree cannot answer it by applying a pattern it already holds. It is phrased as the question and what turns on it, and it is deleted from here when it is folded into the file that owns its subject.

The pass was six stances reading the tree cold, none seeing another's work. Where three of them reached a question separately that is recorded with it, because independent arrival is evidence about the finding rather than about the stance.

## The score and what it decides

**Can the authorship prior ever narrow?** (Three stances.) Three decisions compose against it. There is no tenancy, so every install learns from its own outcomes alone. The prior is per model version and not per family, so moving a fleet entry to a newer version starts its evidence over, on a cadence faster than one install accumulates vetoes and rollbacks. And two of the three learning signals — a veto of an auto-passed change, a gate the factory would have passed but a human rejected — require a human at a gate, so the supply contracts as the score succeeds. [_Deferred_](deferred.md) already describes the endpoint and calls it the design working: a service that has shipped for ten years starts from the same floor as one that has never shipped at all. What turns on it is auto-pass, the shrinking of human work, the trust number, and the claim in the repository's `README.md` that the factory runs on its own.

**Where is the risk threshold defined, and what is its arity?** (Three stances.) [_Gate policy_](how-humans-do-it/09-gate-policy.md) lists it as the first of the seven parameters and links it to [_Risk score_](how-humans-do-it/04-risk-score.md), which never defines it; [_Gates_](how-humans-do-it/03-gates.md) does not mention it. The only substantive statement is in the gathering section, that a gate row's threshold is a field of an environment record and all eight rows of the default path read production's — which makes it per row, so an owner faces eight of them and not one. It also has no pin direction where every other parameter has one, and no starting value where K starts at 1 and the authorship prior starts wide. What turns on it is whether a human decides at any gate at all, which every autonomy claim in the tree routes through.

**Does anything read the trust number?** [_Surfaces_](how-humans-do-it/11-surfaces.md) says Factory has one number that governs trust and that humans decide how much autonomy to allow from it. No parameter carries that decision, no surface offers the action, and [_Gate policy_](how-humans-do-it/09-gate-policy.md) gathers everything an owner authors as seven rows containing no autonomy control. What turns on it is whether the number governs anything or reports, and [_One pipeline_](how-humans-do-it/01-one-pipeline.md) uses it as a shipping precondition — nothing ships that the trust number cannot see — which reads as the first.

## The comparison

**Does the comparison get a liveness record?** (Three stances.) It computes the health signal, opens and closes every watch window, writes every incident, triggers every automatic rollback, and calls intake with what it finds. [_Operations_](how-humans-do-it/08-operations.md) states that a comparison which stops running leaves windows open, fills K, and holds that service's production deploys — a wait on the factory, which does not page, so it shows only as a row in Work. The same file gives the reconciler a **last comparison** record on the grounds that a check which silently stops is worse than the bug it catches, and withholds the equivalent from the component that performs the recovery. A stalled comparison also detects no crossing, so a regressing release is never rolled back, and the reconciler reads a running-but-harmful release as a match. What turns on it is whether the only unattended safety mechanism in the design can fail silently.

**What quantity does the comparison compare, and who makes the software emit it?** (Two stances.) The comparison is defined relationally — a release against a control, on comparable traffic — and the measured quantity is never named. The boundary, the crossing, the incident, the automatic rollback, and the pinned explicit health threshold all read a number nothing defines, and the window's arithmetic asks an owner to author the smallest regression the comparison must rule out for a quantity the tree does not name. Nothing anywhere covers instrumenting the software so that it emits one: the factory writes the code, and no stage, role, or artifact names the work. What turns on it is the whole post-deploy half of the design.

**Does a comparison survive the teardown of its control?** [_Operations_](how-humans-do-it/08-operations.md) says the comparison keeps running after the window closes, and that at the clean and cap exits the control is torn down. Its post-window findings are the tree's answer to three holes it admits elsewhere — the semantic change no diff sees, the consumer that breaks after its producer's window closed, and the consumer already in production when its producer rolls back — and each of those answers is the consumer breaking in its own error rate. What turns on it is whether there is a second detection mechanism the design assumes and never describes.

## Rollback and the trust domain

**Do the deploy gate's holds apply to a rollback?** [_Releases_](how-humans-do-it/06-releases.md) makes a rollback a deploy event, [_Gates_](how-humans-do-it/03-gates.md) puts a gate before each deploy, and the tree resolves only the pin case. If the holds apply, the hold on the service's K windows already open catches every rollback, because a rollback happens while windows are open by definition. If they do not, the rollback also bypasses the reconciler's mismatch hold — and [_Operations_](how-humans-do-it/08-operations.md) names the rollback as the exact action that hold exists to stop, since a rollback targets a release computed from the windows the factory itself closed. What turns on it is whether the recovery deadlocks on its own preconditions or runs against records already shown wrong.

**Does the reconciler read one factory record or two?** (Two stances.) [_Operations_](how-humans-do-it/08-operations.md) says in one place that it reads one factory record, the production deploy record naming each service's current release, and thirteen lines earlier that a build beside the current release is a mismatch only where no open window names it, at the cost of reading two records to interpret one fact. The second reading makes the suppression rule depend on records written by the comparison, so a wrong or stale window record makes the reconciler excuse a real mismatch, and the one component outside the trust domain fails open on evidence from inside it. What turns on it is the independence argument the reconciler exists for.

**Does a harm exit with no rollback target reach a human?** [_Releases_](how-humans-do-it/06-releases.md) says that where a harm exit finds no release to return to the condemned release keeps serving, the comparison raises the revert intent itself, and the escalation page on that item is the only thing that reaches a human before it ships. That page fires only when the attempt bound is exceeded, so where the factory succeeds at building the revert, a release the factory has condemned serves live traffic for a full pipeline cycle with nothing pushed to anyone. What turns on it is whether the page list in [_Operations_](how-humans-do-it/08-operations.md) is missing a fourth condition.

**What happens when the record graph is lost?** (Two stances.) Master's head, the next number, current release, the restore floor, criteria in force, declarations in force, and the deprecation list are each a query and nothing writes any of them, every removal argued on its own as avoiding a fact written twice. Together they mean that after a loss or a partial restore nothing can say which release is running, which item produced it, or what number comes next — and the next fast-forward mints a number already used. Two further cases sit with it: records written by one version of the product must be readable by the next, since the owner upgrades a self-hosted install, and the build artifact a slow rollback redeploys is never given a home or a lifetime. What turns on it is whether one graph on the customer's disk is a decision the design has costed.

## Dispatch and the pipeline

**What dispatches the interview and the cut?** (Two stances.) Dispatch is a match of the item's stage against the role and the item's service and area against the scope, and items do not exist until the cut writes them. Both upstream stages spend agent time — [_The fleet_](how-humans-do-it/10-fleet.md) has intake writing what the interview spent at each round — so an agent ran on a credential with no entry that could have matched it. The cut is scored like every other gate, and the authorship factor is per author, so it has a scored author with no way to have been assigned one. What turns on it is whether the pipeline can start.

**Where does a reject send an item?** [_Gates_](how-humans-do-it/03-gates.md) says reject sends the change back up the pipeline and does not say how far. A merge-gate rejection whose feedback is that the spec was wrong and one whose feedback is that the code was wrong cannot land in the same place, and the target decides which stage's attempt count increments, which decides when the item escalates. No actor is named for the choice. Alongside it: an agent resuming after an exhausted credential is told to count nothing against the bound, but the item records stage, attempts, and spend and no partial progress, so a resume is a restart.

**What is an area's declaration made of?** [_What humans do_](what-humans-do.md) calls an area an owner-declared grouping of the software, coarser than a file and free to cut across services, and [_Intent into items_](how-humans-do-it/02-intent-into-items.md) has the cut write the narrowest area in the chain whose declaration covers the work. The declaration's content is never given, so covering the work has no decidable form. Its readers are the cut, dispatch on the write path of every item, every pin drawn on an area, the item-size target, and the score's area churn factor. If the declaration is prose, the guarantee that an owner cannot narrow their way out of a pin holds only as a model judgment. What turns on it is whether pins bind.

## The candidate run and the substrate

**What is inside a candidate environment, and what drives its run?** (Two stances.) Three of the four mechanical pre-merge checks are decided there — each acceptance criterion, every consumer's declaration checked against the exchanges observed there, and a predicate-form constraint decided against an observed run. An exchange needs a caller and a run needs data, and [_Environments_](how-humans-do-it/05-environments.md) says a candidate's environment holds nothing an owner authored, so it has no credential reference and the software in it reaches nothing that needs one. The same file's standard elsewhere is that a comparison computed from one human exercising a screen is noise that looks like evidence. What turns on it is whether the merge gate's verdict is evidence, and the score learns from that verdict.

**Who makes a place for the customer's software to run?** [_What humans do_](what-humans-do.md) names three things outside the twelve duties — hosting the factory, installing the reconciler, composing the fleet — and making a runtime is not among them. On the factory's side the deploy agent creates candidate environments and is the only component reaching substrate, while an owner declares every target before the factory can deploy into an environment, and those two cannot both hold for an environment the deploy agent creates at gate approval. The cut can also create a whole new service, with nothing said about a repository host, a store, or an address for its first release. The one hold the tree does state — a substrate with no room for another environment — implies a capacity nobody supplies or sizes. What turns on it is the first candidate deploy.

## What the tree never raises

**Is there any way to put a change in production without exposing it?** One item per release, no batching, and every merged release takes live traffic, so a user-visible feature the cut yields four items for is visible to end users in four increments. [_Intent into items_](how-humans-do-it/02-intent-into-items.md) describes the resulting state — a partial intent is a feature half delivered, not production half broken — without naming the mechanism that ordinarily prevents it. The subject is never raised anywhere, which distinguishes it from the alternatives the tree names and refuses with a reason. What turns on it is whether undo after end users have seen it is the only lever for an unwanted feature.

**Can the factory see the dependencies of the software it builds?** Intake's three sources are closed — an owner's request, grouped end-user reports, and a detector — and a detector is a comparison still running after its window closed or a consumer breaking in its own error rate. None of the three can raise a published vulnerability or a new major version, no gate rejects a candidate for adding a known-bad or license-incompatible dependency, and an agent writing code adds dependencies routinely with no human reading the diff once the score has settled. What turns on it is the claim that the factory runs a codebase for years and fixes its own bugs.

**Is there a stop wider than one subject?** Every undo the tree holds — hold, reject, rollback, revert, veto after the fact, withdrawing a constraint, withdrawing a pin — is scoped to one item, service, or area. Nothing is scoped to the factory, no mechanism reverts a score version or bounds how far one may move from the last, and the response the design prescribes is pinning gates, which is per subject and must be authored before anyone knows which subject. What turns on it is the failure the design has no answer to: the factory going wrong the same way across many items at once.

**Can an owner withdraw an intent or stop a healthy item?** A constraint is withdrawn by a second write and a pin records whether it has been withdrawn. Intent is the one owner-supplied record with no withdrawal, and the only route to `dropped` requires the factory to fail first — a human stopping an item that escalated and nobody took over. So an owner who changes their mind after the cut, or sees a wrong cut that has exceeded no bound, watches it merge, mint a number, deploy, and be undone by a revert item. What turns on it is the most frequent event in software delivery.

**What is untrusted about an end-user report?** The report way in is thin and embedded in the deployed software, the grouper reads the words to decide whether two reports are one problem, each group raises an intent immediately, and for a grouped intent the reports are what the interview and the spec are authored against. In the steady state the score auto-passes every gate downstream of that, so there is a described path from anonymous free text to production code with no human in it. Volume is the other half: five hundred distinct reports are five hundred intents each with its own attempt bound, and [_The fleet_](how-humans-do-it/10-fleet.md) refuses a spend ceiling on principle. What turns on it is whether the one channel open to the outside is authenticated or rated at all.

**What retires a service, project, area, or environment?** The tree designs creation and lifecycle for each and stops at the end of each one's useful life, and it already names one orphan it creates — a service record the cut wrote for an item the re-cut did not replace. A retired service's production targets are a mismatch the reconciler pages on indefinitely, and that hold does not lift itself. A dead consumer keeps declaring what its last release read, so a producer's deprecation list never empties and its removal item is never raised. What turns on it is whether three named mechanisms run forever against records nobody can retire.

## Routing, batching, and the quiet service

**Who receives a gate row a pin created?** (Two stances.) [_What humans do_](what-humans-do.md) opens the duty list with a compliance officer holding duty 2 and a pin over a regulated area, and [_Surfaces_](how-humans-do-it/11-surfaces.md) routes a row to the duty the tree names where one is named — confirming criteria at Spec, UAT at the merge gate — and to the owner everywhere else, with a pin adding the human and not changing who it is. Only two of the eight rows have a duty attached, so the compliance officer authors a pin at the production deploy gate and never sees a row from it. What turns on it is whether delegation works for two gates or for all of them, and the thirteenth duty the tree refuses is the stated reason it cannot.

**What happens to a change that genuinely cannot be decomposed?** [_Contracts_](how-humans-do-it/07-contracts.md) says that is an escalation and not permission to batch, that taking it over gives a human no power the factory lacks, and that what the escalation asks for is a re-cut. The premise is that decomposition is impossible for this change and the conclusion is that a decomposition is requested, with no mechanism between them, so the item exceeds the bound, escalates, is cleared, and escalates again. Two candidates for the case sit in the tree already: a store change whose data rather than schema breaks the forward promise, and two services whose synchronous protocol changes in both directions at once. What turns on it is whether the tree's most load-bearing rule has a terminal state other than `dropped`.

**Does the watch window work on a low-volume service?** The traffic a comparison needs scales as the inverse square of what it must detect, and [_Operations_](how-humans-do-it/08-operations.md) concedes that a service too quiet to receive that much ends at the cap every time, where the window is weak protection and is reported as weak. A self-hosted single-customer install runs mostly internal services, so that is the ordinary case rather than an edge one — and the cap exit, which the exit table calls closed unresolved, then raises K and sets the restore floor. So on the services with the weakest measurement, K climbs on windows that resolved nothing and a rollback returns to the newest release the factory learned nothing about. What turns on it is whether the window's output is valid input downstream.

**Can a fix for an incident reach production ahead of the queue?** [_Environments_](how-humans-do-it/05-environments.md) says the merge queue orders nothing after the merge, that numbered releases waiting to deploy are ordered by the number and by nothing else, and that an urgent fix behind three numbered releases waits for them, with the revert a rollback waits on as the sole exception. Everything the comparison did not catch inside a window becomes an ordinary item taking the same stages and gates, so it merges at N+4 and waits for three watch windows, K at a time, each a full cap on a quiet service. Priority orders nothing after the merge, approving does not reorder, and a pin only adds. What turns on it is the claim that the factory fixes its own bugs.

**Is the decision log append-only, or are its records written twice?** [_Deferred_](deferred.md) describes one append-only log with each record chaining its predecessor, and [_Gates_](how-humans-do-it/03-gates.md) makes a gate decision one record with two writes at two events, opened at the firing and closed at the verdict. A record mutated after later records have chained onto it is not an append-only chain, and the analogy the tree offers — the deploy record advancing its status — is not in the log. The held-out flag adds a third write. Alongside it, the merge queue's rejection is written where a decision is written with the queue as the actor, and a queue rejection has no gate row and no firing, which makes it a fourth shape in a log the tree enumerates as holding three. What turns on it is whether seam 2 delivers evidence or a mutable table.

**Is a schema diff enough for the promise a rollback rests on?** [_Releases_](how-humans-do-it/06-releases.md) says that what the newer release wrote while it was live is still readable by the one being restored, and that this is not luck. [_Contracts_](how-humans-do-it/07-contracts.md) enforces it with a diff of the schema a candidate declares against the one in production, and states two files over that a diff proves the shape is unchanged and not that callers still work. The promise is also about data the new release writes once it is live, which does not exist when the candidate runs, and it is pairwise while a rollback undoes every release above its target up to K. What turns on it is rollback as a reliable recovery, which is the premise licensing auto-pass.
